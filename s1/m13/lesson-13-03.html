Lesson 13.03
★ Ten Verbs, Four Parts Each — and the English Inside
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You’ve met most of these verbs already — today you learn their full four parts, and discover how much English they’ve been quietly handing you.
These are the workhorse verbs of Roman public life: to say, to do, to lead, to write, to send, to conquer. Learn each one’s four parts and you get two gifts at once — the power to build both its present and its perfect, and a whole shelf of English words. Learn dīcō and you’ve explained dictate, diction, predict, verdict, and dictionary in a single stroke.

I will be able to give the meaning and the third principal part (perfect) of this module’s ten verbs.
2
I will be able to connect each Latin verb to at least one English word that descends from it.

📚 PART 2 · THE TEN VERBS 15 min

Full four parts — and the English they carry

The first eight are pure 3rd conjugation. The last two — faciō and capiō — are the common 3rd-iō verbs; their perfects behave exactly like the rest. The third part is the one to burn in — it is your perfect.

dīcō, dīcere, dīxī, dictumto say, speakdictate, diction, predict, verdict

dūcō, dūcere, dūxī, ductumto leadconduct, produce, educate, aqueduct
scrībō, scrībere, scrīpsī, scrīptumto writescribe, script, inscription, manuscript
mittō, mittere, mīsī, missumto sendmission, transmit, missile, dismiss
legō, legere, lēgī, lēctumto read, chooselegible, lecture, legend, select

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

One verb, a whole shelf of English

🌱 FROM FACIŌ (TO MAKE, TO DO)

The fourth principal part factum is a Latin word meaning “a thing done.” English borrowed it whole as fact. A factory is a place where things are made; to manufacture is literally “to make by hand” (manū + factum). Even affect and effect hide the same root. One Roman verb, and a shelf of English falls open.

This is why Latin verbs pay compound interest: each is a root English has grown a dozen words from. You’re not memorizing ten verbs today — you’re unlocking scores of English ones.

Now go the other way: capture, captive, and capable come from capiō. What does capiō mean, and what is its perfect (“he took”)? Say it, then reveal.

capiō means “to take, seize.” Its perfect is cēpit — “he took / he seized” (from the third part cēpī). Notice the vowel change from capit (he takes) to cēpit (he took) — a long ē marks the past.
