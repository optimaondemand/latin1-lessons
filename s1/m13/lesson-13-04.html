★ Reading Latin — Cicero on the Rōstra
Lesson · about 42 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Now you can read across time. Today a single sentence can happen now or have happened then — and you can tell which.
First you read connected Latin that mixes the present and the perfect, watching the verb stem to know when the action happens. Then you climb onto the Rōstra — the speaker’s platform in the Roman Forum, built from the bronze beaks of captured warships, where Cicero made his name… and where, in the end, his enemies displayed his head. No new grammar today — today you use what you built.

⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 14 min

Sentences across time

Read each aloud. Find the subject, then the verb — and check the stem: a present stem means now, a perfect stem means then. The gray line is your parse and translation; try it yourself before you read it.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Before you read: when you meet a 3rd conjugation verb in a sentence, what will you look at first to tell now from then?
Cicerō ōrātor est.

Cicero is an orator. — Cicerō nom. sg. · ōrātor (orator) predicate nom. · est present of esse.

Hodiē Cicerō in Forō dīcit.

Today Cicero speaks in the Forum. — hodiē = “today” · in Forō abl. (in the Forum) · dīcit present 3rd sg. (present stem dīc-).

Herī Cicerō in Rōstrīs dīxit.

Yesterday Cicero spoke on the Rōstra. — herī = “yesterday” · in Rōstrīs abl. (on the speaker’s platform) · dīxit perfect 3rd sg. (perfect stem dīx-).

Rōmānī Cicerōnem laudāvērunt.

The Romans praised Cicero. — Rōmānī nom. pl. (the Romans) · Cicerōnem acc. sg. · laudāvērunt perfect 3rd pl.

Cicerō librum scrīpsit et populō lēgit.

Cicero wrote a book and read it to the people. — librum acc. sg. (book) · scrīpsit perfect · populō dat. sg. (to the people) · lēgit perfect (note the long ē: lēgit = “read,” not legit = “reads”).

Mīlitēs urbem cēpērunt et hostēs vīcērunt.

The soldiers took the city and conquered the enemy. — mīlitēs nom. pl. · urbem acc. sg. (city) · cēpērunt perfect of capiō · hostēs acc. pl. (enemies) · vīcērunt perfect of vincō.

💡 TWO SIGNPOSTS FOR TIME

The little words hodiē (today) and herī (yesterday) are your friends: they tell you which tense to expect before you even reach the verb. But the real proof is always in the stem — dīcit vs. dīxit.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ THE RŌSTRA · THE VOICE OF THE FORUM

In the heart of the Roman Forum stood a raised stone platform where speakers addressed the crowd. In 338 BCE, Rome crushed the fleet of the coastal town of Antium and stripped the bronze rams — the sharp rōstra (“beaks”) that warships used to smash enemy hulls — from the captured vessels. The Romans bolted those war-trophies to the front of the platform. From then on the platform itself carried their name: the Rōstra.

To speak from the Rōstra was to hold Rome’s attention. Here Cicero — the greatest orator of the Republic — warned the people against those who would seize power for themselves. Words spoken here could raise a man to consul or bring a conspirator down.

But words made enemies. After Cicero attacked Mark Antony in a series of blistering speeches, Antony had him hunted down and killed in 43 BCE. On Antony’s order, Cicero’s head and hands — the hands that had written the speeches — were nailed to the Rōstra, in the very place from which he had spoken. The platform that had carried his voice now displayed his silence.

⚖️ COURAGE · THE COST OF SPEAKING TRUTH TO POWER

Cicero knew Antony was dangerous, and he spoke anyway. The Romans prized virtūs — courage — and they remembered Cicero not only for how well he spoke but for what it cost him. To speak the truth to someone who can end you is one of the oldest tests of courage, and the Rōstra is its monument.

In your own words: what does the story of the Rōstra — from ships’ beaks to Cicero’s head — say about the power and the danger of public speech in Rome? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

The Rōstra was where words won victories — it was literally built from military ones. Cicero used it to shape Rome by speaking, which made him powerful and made him enemies. When Antony displayed his head there, it was a message: speech can lift you up and it can get you killed. The platform holds both truths at once.
📜 ETYMOLOGY MOMENT
🌱 FROM RŌSTRA TO THE MODERN LECTERN

English kept the word: a rostrum is the lectern or platform a speaker stands at — a museum guide, a conductor, a graduation speaker. Every time someone “steps up to the rostrum,” they are standing, in name at least, on the beaks of ships Rome captured in 338 BCE. And the verb of the platform, dīcō (to say), still speaks through dictate, diction, and predict.
