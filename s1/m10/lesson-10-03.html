★ Eight Verbs — the Vocabulary of Power

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have the pattern. Today you get the words — and you'll find you half-know them from English already.
This module's eight verbs are all third conjugation, so yesterday's paradigm runs each of them. They are also, almost every one, a root beneath dozens of English words. Learn scrībere and you've explained scribe, script, describe, inscription, and manuscript at once. Learn dīcere and you unlock dictate, predict, contradict, and dictionary. Today: meet the eight, and pull the English out of each.

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

One verb, a whole shelf of English

🌱 FROM SCRĪBERE (TO WRITE)

Scrībere gives English scribe (one who writes) and script (something written) directly. Add a prefix and it multiplies: de‑scribe (write down), in‑scription (writing carved into stone), manu‑script (written by hand), pre‑scription (written before). When a doctor prescribes or an editor asks for your manuscript, they are reaching for a word Roman clerks used two thousand years ago.

This is why Latin vocabulary pays compound interest: each verb is a root that English has grown a dozen words from. You're not memorizing eight words today — you're unlocking scores of English ones.

Now go the other way: component, deposit, and postpone come from pōnere. What does pōnere mean, and why does deposit fit? Say it, then reveal.

“To put, place.” To deposit money is to put it down for safekeeping; a component is a part placed together with others; to postpone is to place after (later). All point straight back to pōnere — Latin roots turn English into a set of hints.
