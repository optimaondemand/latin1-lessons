Lesson 10.01
★ The Third Family — Rome's Verbs of Action
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You already know two verb families. This week you meet the third — and it's the one Rome's greatest speakers reached for most.
Back in Module 5 you learned the first conjugation (-āre); last module you added the second (-ēre). Today the third conjugation arrives — and it holds the verbs that make Roman public life move: to say, to write, to lead, to send, to conquer. These are the verbs of the Forum, where an orator like Cicero could change history with a single speech. Today there is nothing to drill — just one idea to lock in: how to recognize the third family, and why it hides behind a sneaky lookalike ending.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a third conjugation verb by its infinitive ending -ere (short e).
2
I will be able to tell the third conjugation apart from the second, whose ending -ēre looks almost identical.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

Two families down

You can already run two whole conjugations. Each is known by the ending on its infinitive — its “to ___” dictionary form.

amāre

to love — first conjugation, ending in -āre (long ā).

vidēre

to see — second conjugation, ending in -ēre (long ē).

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Three families, three name tags

Every Latin verb belongs to a conjugation, and the infinitive ending is its badge. Here are the three you'll know by the end of this week, side by side:

ConjugationInfinitive endingExampleMeaning
First-āre (long ā)amāreto love
Second-ēre (long ē)vidēreto see
Third-ere (short e)agereto do, drive

The third conjugation is the largest and busiest family in Latin. Its members are the everyday engines of action: dīcere (to say), scrībere (to write), dūcere (to lead), mittere (to send), vincere (to conquer). When a Roman senator stood to speak, argue, and persuade, nearly every verb in his sentence came from this family.

💡 WHY IT MATTERS THIS QUARTER

Our theme this quarter is the art of Roman rhetoric — the craft of persuasion. Rhetoric is doing things with words: you dīcis (you speak), you scrībis (you write), you dūcis (you lead). The third conjugation is, quite literally, the grammar of Roman power.

✅ PART 3 · THE LOOKALIKE TRAP 18 min

Short e vs. long ē

Here's the one thing to watch. The second conjugation ends in -ēre with a long ē (marked with a macron). The third ends in -ere with a short e (no macron). Same three letters — one small mark makes all the difference.

Second — -ēre

vidēre — to see
habēre — to have

Long ē. The stress sits on that ē: vi‑DĒ‑re.

Third — -ere

agere — to do
dūcere — to lead

Short e. The stress jumps back: Á‑ge‑re, DŪ‑ce‑re.

This is why macrons are worth reading closely: the little bar over a vowel is not decoration — here it is the whole difference between two verb families. In this course, when you see -ere with no macron, think third conjugation.
