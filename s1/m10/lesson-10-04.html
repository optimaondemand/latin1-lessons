★ Reading Latin — The Night Cicero Stood Up
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Pattern plus verbs equals reading. Today the pieces click together — on one of history's most famous speeches.
First you read real Latin sentences using this week's third-conjugation verbs. Then you step into the year 63 BCE, when a conspirator named Catiline plotted to seize Rome by force — and the consul Cicero rose in the Senate to stop him with nothing but words. It is the perfect story for our quarter's theme: rhetoric as action, speech as a weapon. No new grammar today: today you use what you built.

⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the verb, and check that they agree in number. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Before you read: our theme is rhetoric — the art of persuasion. What do you think a Roman valued most in a great speaker?
Cicerō dīcit; Catilīna timet.

Cicero speaks; Catiline is afraid. — dīcit 3rd sg. (3rd conj.) · timet 3rd sg. (2nd conj., from Module 9). Two families in one line.

Cicerō Catilīnam nōn amat.

Cicero does not love Catiline. — Cicerō nom. sg. (subject) · Catilīnam acc. sg. (object; Catilīna is a 1st-decl. name) · amat 3rd sg.

Cicerō verba scrībit.

Cicero writes words. — verba acc. pl. (“words”) · scrībit 3rd sg. of scrībere.

Cicerō Catilīnam ex oppidō agit.

Cicero drives Catiline out of the town. — agit here = “drives” · ex oppidō abl. (“out of the town,” from Module 8).

Rōmānī Catilīnam vincunt.

The Romans conquer Catiline. — Rōmānī nom. pl. (“the Romans”) · vincunt 3rd pl. of vincere — note the -unt vowel.

💡 NOTICE · MIXED CONJUGATIONS

Real Latin mixes verb families in a single sentence. In the first line, dīcit (3rd conj.) sits right beside timet (2nd conj.). Both are third person singular — the ending tells you that, no matter which family the verb comes from. That's the payoff of learning the endings so well.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ THE CATILINARIAN CONSPIRACY · ROME, 63 BCE

Lucius Sergius Catiline (Catilīna) was a ruined aristocrat, deep in debt and hungry for power. Twice defeated in elections for consul, he gathered other desperate men and hatched a plot: to murder the leading men of Rome, burn parts of the city, and seize the Republic by force.

The consul that year was Cicero — not born to a great family, but the finest speaker Rome ever produced. Through informants he learned every detail of the plot. On the 8th of November, with Catiline himself seated in the Senate, Cicero rose and delivered the speech we call the First Catilinarian. He named the conspiracy aloud, laid the evidence bare, and told Catiline to his face that Rome already knew everything.

No sword was drawn. Cicero drove Catiline out of the city with words. Humiliated and exposed, Catiline fled that very night to join his army in the countryside. Within weeks the rebellion was crushed and Catiline was killed in battle. Cicero was hailed as pater patriae — “father of the fatherland.” It was the moment that proved a Roman could conquer with speech alone.

💬 THE MOST FAMOUS OPENING IN LATIN

Cicero began with a line every Latin student eventually meets:

“Quō usque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā?”

— “How long, Catiline, will you keep abusing our patience?” The grammar is beyond us for now (we'll admire it, not parse it), but hear the nerve of it: the consul opens not with a greeting but with an accusation, hurled at the guilty man sitting right in front of him. A few lines later comes another phrase still quoted today: Ō tempora! Ō mōrēs! — “Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!”

In your own words: why is this night remembered as a triumph of rhetoric in particular — not of battle? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Cicero defeated a violent conspiracy without an army — he used only evidence, timing, and the power of a well-made speech. By naming the plot openly and shaming Catiline before the Senate, he made it impossible for the conspiracy to continue. That is rhetoric as action: words that do something in the world. It's why Rome remembered the speech, not a sword.
📜 ETYMOLOGY MOMENT
🌱 FROM DĪCERE & DŪCERE — THE ROOTS OF LEADERSHIP

Cicero's whole career lived on two of this week's verbs. Dīcere (to say) gives us diction, dictate, and predict — and a verdict is literally a “true saying” (vērum + dictum). Dūcere (to lead) gives us conduct, produce, and educate — to educate is to “lead out” (ē- + dūcere) a mind toward what is true. A great orator did both at once: he said the right words and thereby led a nation.
