★ The Sounds That Trip Up English Speakers
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Most Latin consonants sound just like English. A handful do not — and those are the ones that give you away.
If you pronounce Caesar as “SEE-zer,” a Roman would blink. He said “KYE-sar.” Today you learn the small set of letters Latin plays by its own rules — c and g are always hard, v is really a “w,” consonant-i is a “y,” and r is rolled — plus the three diphthongs, vowel-pairs like ae and au that blend into one sound. Master these and the names in the Aeneas story will finally sound Roman.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to pronounce the tricky consonants c, g, v, consonant-i, and r the Roman way.
2
I will be able to read the three main diphthongs ae, oe, and au.
3
I will be able to apply these sounds to Roman names like Caesar, Aenēās, and Venus.

📚 PART 2 · THE TRICKY CONSONANTS 12 min

The letters that don't behave like English

Most consonants (b, d, f, l, m, n, p…) sound just as you'd expect. Here are the ones that don't — learn these and you've learned the hard part.

LetterSoundExample
cAlways hard “k,” even before e or i — never soft like “city”Cicerō = “KIK-er-oh”
gAlways hard “g” as in “go” — never soft like “gem”gemma = “GHEM-ma”
vLike English “w”vīnum = “WEE-noom”
i (consonant)Before a vowel, like English “y”iam = “yam”; Iūlius = “YOO-lee-oos”
rTrilled / rolled with the tongue-tip, like Spanish or Italian rRōma
sAlways the hissed “s” of “sing” — never a “z”rosa = “ROH-sah”
tAlways a clean “t” — never “sh”nātiō = “NAH-tee-oh,” not “nay-shun”
💡 THE GREEK PAIRS

In words borrowed from Greek you'll see ch, ph, and th. Classically these are just k, p, and t with a puff of breath — so ph is not the English “f,” and th is not the English “th” of “this.” Example: Carthāgō = “car-THAH-goh.”

📌 PART 3 · THE THREE DIPHTHONGS 8 min

Two vowels, one sound

A diphthong is a pair of vowels pronounced as a single glide — one sound, one syllable. Latin has three you'll meet constantly:

DiphthongSounds like…Example
aethe “eye” in aisle / highCaesar (KYE-sar), Aenēās
oethe “oy” in boypoena (POY-nah, “penalty”)
authe “ow” in cow / howaurum (OW-room, “gold”), nauta

You'll occasionally see ei, eu, and ui as diphthongs too, but ae, oe, and au are the three that matter most. The key rule: when you see one of these pairs, don't say two separate vowels — glide them into a single sound.
