📚 PART 2 · THE ROMAN WORLD 10 min

Where and when Latin lived

Latin began as the local speech of Latium, the small region around the city of Rome, founded (by tradition) in 753 BC. As Rome grew from a village into an empire, its language traveled with its roads, armies, and laws — until Latin was spoken from Britain to North Africa. For over a thousand years afterward it remained the shared language of Western scholarship, law, and the Church.

📜 PART 3 · WHY LATIN STILL MATTERS 8 min

A “dead” language that never stopped working

People call Latin “dead” because no country speaks it as a native tongue anymore. But it is doing more work today than most living languages:

  • Medicine — the body's parts and most diagnoses carry Latin names: femur, cranium, vertebra.
  • Law — courts still argue in Latin phrases: habeas corpus, pro bono, subpoena.
  • Science — every plant and animal has a two-part Latin name: Homo sapiens, Canis lupus.
  • Language — and, most of all, Latin never truly died. It turned into new languages.
🌱 THE MOTHER OF THE ROMANCE LANGUAGES

As spoken Latin spread and drifted, it slowly became five national languages — the Romance languages (from Rōmānicus, “in the Roman manner”): Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian. Watch one Latin word become all five:

LatinIts descendants
aqua (water)Spanish agua · Italian acqua · French eau · Portuguese água

English isn't a Romance language, but it borrowed enormously from Latin — some words directly (video, agenda, exit) and thousands more through French after the Norman Conquest of 1066 (royal, justice, city). Well over half of English vocabulary traces back to Latin.

Read these three names from the Aeneas story aloud — Aenēās, Dīdō, Rōma. Mark the stressed syllable in each and note one tricky sound. Then reveal.

Aenēās = ai-NĒ-ās — the ae is a diphthong (“eye”); penult nē is long, so it takes the stress. Dīdō = DĪ-dō — two syllables, stress the first; both vowels are long. Rōma = RŌ-ma — two syllables, stress the first; the r is trilled.
