Lesson 0.01
★ The Latin Alphabet — Almost Yours Already
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Welcome to Latin. You already know almost every letter you'll ever need — because ours came straight from theirs.
The alphabet on this page is the one the Romans carved on their monuments and the one you are reading right now. It is nearly identical to English — with a few small, telling differences. Today there is nothing to memorize and no drilling: just meet the 23 letters, notice the three English letters the Romans did without, and learn the clever double lives of I and V. By Friday you'll be reading the names in the story of Aeneas out loud.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the 23 letters of the classical Latin alphabet and the letters English has that Latin lacked.
2
I will be able to explain the special jobs of I and V and why K, Y, and Z are rare.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

A head start you didn't know you had

Look at these three Roman words the way you'd look at English: Rōma, nauta, aqua. You can already sound out every letter. That's not luck — our alphabet is the Roman one, borrowed and lightly adjusted over two thousand years.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Glancing at those three words, how much of the Latin alphabet feels familiar to you?
📚 PART 2 · THE 23 LETTERS 12 min

Twenty-three, not twenty-six

The classical Latin alphabet had 23 letters. Here they are:

A B C D E F G H I K L M N O P Q R S T V X Y Z

Compare that to English's 26. Three of our letters simply weren't there:

English has…What Rome did instead
JNo such letter. Latin used I for both the vowel and the “y”-consonant — the letter J was invented much later.
URome wrote one letter, V, for both the “oo” vowel and the “w” consonant. The rounded u shape came later. (This course prints u and v separately to make reading easier.)
WNever existed in Latin at all — its name even admits it: “double-u.”

And a few letters that Rome technically had were used only rarely:

LetterWhy it's rare
KAlmost never used — C already covered the “k” sound. It survives in a handful of old words like Kalendae (the Kalends).
YBorrowed from Greek, and used almost only in words taken from Greek, such as Aegyptus (Egypt).
ZAlso a Greek import, appearing only in Greek loanwords like zōna (belt, zone).
💡 ONE ALPHABET, MANY DESCENDANTS

The letters you're reading are called the Roman or Latin alphabet for a reason: English, Spanish, French, Italian, and dozens of other languages all write with Rome's letters. When you learn to read Latin, you're reading the source code of half the world's writing.

✅ PART 3 · THE DOUBLE-LIFE LETTERS & A QUICK CHECK 15 min

I and V do two jobs each

Because Rome had no J, W, or separate U, two letters carried a double load:

The letter I

As a vowel, it's the “ee” in īnsula (island).

As a consonant (before a vowel), it sounds like English “y”: Iūlius = “YOO-lee-us,” iam = “yam.”

The letter V

As a vowel (written u here), it's the “oo” sound, as in unda (wave).

As a consonant, it sounds like English “w”: Venus = “WE-nus.” (More on this in Lesson 0.03.)

Don't worry about producing these sounds perfectly yet — that's the rest of the week. For now, just get comfortable with the shape of the alphabet. Work through the checks below; wrong answers give a hint, and Reset lets you try again.

✅ TRY IT · true or false

Out loud, name the three letters English uses that classical Latin did without — and say what Latin used instead of each. Then reveal.

J, U, and W. For “J,” Latin used I (as a consonant); for “U,” it used V (as a vowel); and “W” simply didn't exist. Everything else on your English keyboard, the Romans would recognize.
