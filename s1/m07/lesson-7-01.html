Lesson 7.01
★ The Fourth Case — To and For
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can already say who acts, who gets acted on, and whose something is. Today a fourth case joins them — the one that answers to whom? and for whom?
When someone gives, tells, or shows something, there is almost always a person on the receiving end. Latin has a special case just for that person: the dative. There is no paradigm to memorize today and nothing to drill — just one idea to make friends with. This week's story is Aeneas's journey into the Underworld, a place full of gifts offered to the gods and prophecies given to a hero. By Friday you'll spot the “to/for” case on sight.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what an indirect object is and how English marks it.
2
I will be able to recognize when a Latin sentence needs the dative — the “to / for” case.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

A sentence with three jobs

Read this English sentence and feel out its parts: “Aeneas gives the gods a gift.” Someone does the giving, something is given, and someone receives it. Watch the third one — that receiver is what today is all about.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
In “Aeneas gives the gods a gift,” which words feel like the ones receiving the gift?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 13 min

Four jobs, four cases

You have already met three cases. Each one answers a different question about a noun's job in the sentence. The dative is the fourth — and it fills a gap the others can't.

CaseJob in the sentenceQuestion it answers
Nominativesubject — the doerwho does it?
Genitivepossession — “of”whose is it?
Accusativedirect object — acted uponwhat receives the action?
Dative (new)indirect object — “to / for”to or for whom?

A direct object is the thing acted on. An indirect object is the person who benefits — the one the action heads toward. Compare:

Direct object only

“Aeneas gives a gift.”
Just the thing given. No receiver named.

Add an indirect object

“Aeneas gives the gods a gift.”
Now we know to whom — that's the dative.
💡 ENGLISH HIDES IT

English has two ways to show an indirect object. Sometimes it uses the little word to: “I give the book to my friend.” Other times it just slides the receiver in front of the thing: “I give my friend the book.” Same meaning, no to at all. Latin never needs that little word — the dative ending does the whole job.

✅ PART 3 · SPOT THE RECEIVER 15 min

Train your eye on the indirect object

No Latin forms yet — just learn to find the receiver, because that's the word Latin will put in the dative. Work through all five. Wrong answers give a hint; use Reset to try again.

✅ TRY IT · find the receiver
“Marcus gives his mother flowers.” Which is the indirect object (the receiver)?
✅ TRY IT · name the question
Which question does the dative case answer?
✅ TRY IT · find the receiver
“The poet tells the girl a story.” Which word is the indirect object?
✅ TRY IT · name the case
Which case names the subject — the one doing the action?
✅ TRY IT · spot the “to” phrase
“I gave the book to my friend.” Which part would Latin put in the dative?

Now put all three cases together. In “Aeneas gives the gods a gift,” name the subject, the direct object, and the indirect object. Say your answer, then reveal.

Subject: Aeneas (nominative — the doer). Direct object: a gift (accusative — the thing given). Indirect object: the gods (dative — the receiver, the “to whom”). Three jobs, three different cases — and tomorrow you'll learn the dative endings that mark that third one.
