📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned to spot the family. Today the family learns to speak.
A first conjugation verb can say six different things — I love, you love, he/she/it loves, we love, you all love, they love — and it does it by swapping one small ending. Today you learn the present-tense paradigm: one stem plus six endings. This is the single most useful pattern in the whole module, and it repeats for hundreds of verbs. Learn it once here and the rest of the week is applying it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find a first conjugation verb's present stem by dropping -re from the infinitive.
2
I will be able to add the six personal endings -ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt to build the full present tense.
3
I will be able to explain why the verb ending already tells you the subject.

📚 PART 2 · FIND THE STEM 7 min

Step one: get the stem

To conjugate any first conjugation verb, start from the infinitive and drop the -re. What's left is the present stem — the piece you build on.

InfinitiveDrop -rePresent stem
amāreamāreamā-
portāreportāreportā-
vocārevocārevocā-

Notice the long ā that stays behind. That vowel is the signature sound of the whole family — it shows up in almost every form.

✅ PART 3 · ADD THE ENDINGS 18 min

Step two: the six endings

Add the six personal endings to the stem. Here is the model verb amāre (to love), fully conjugated in the present tense:

SingularPlural
1st personamō — I loveamāmus — we love
2nd personamās — you loveamātis — you all love
3rd personamat — he/she/it lovesamant — they love
💡 THE ONE ODD FORM

Watch the 1st person singular: amāre → stem amā- → amō. The long ā gets swallowed by the -ō ending. It's the only form where the ā disappears — every other form keeps it visible.

These same six endings work for every first conjugation verb. Learn them once:

EndingPerson & numberEnglish subject
-ō1st singularI
-s2nd singularyou
-t3rd singularhe / she / it
-mus1st pluralwe
-tis2nd pluralyou all
-nt3rd pluralthey

Now put the pattern to work — five reps, building forms and reading endings both directions. Keep the chart above in view if you need it; the goal is to make the endings automatic.

📌 PART 4 · A LATIN SUPERPOWER 8 min

The ending is the subject

Here is something English can't do. In Latin, the ending already contains the subject — so a single word can be a complete sentence.

Labōrāmus.

We are working. — the -mus ending means “we,” so no separate word for “we” is needed.

Cantant.

They are singing. — -nt = “they.”

Latin has words for “I” and “we” (ego, nōs), but they're optional — used only for emphasis. The ending does the work.

Your turn: amō is a complete Latin sentence. What does it mean, all by itself? Say it, then reveal.

“I love.” The -ō ending is the “I.” One word, one full sentence — subject and verb together.
