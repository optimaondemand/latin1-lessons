📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Pattern plus verbs equals reading. Today the pieces click together — and the story turns tragic.
First you read real Latin sentences, using the paradigm and the eight verbs from this week. Then you meet Dido, queen of Carthage, who falls in love with Aeneas — and the Roman idea that a hero's duty can cost him the thing he loves. No new grammar today: today you use what you built.

⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the verb, and check that they agree in number. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it.

Nauta aquam portat.

The sailor carries the water. — nauta nom. sg. (subject) · aquam acc. sg. (object) · portat 3rd sg.

Puellae fābulam narrant.

The girls tell a story. — puellae nom. pl. · fābulam acc. sg. · narrant 3rd pl.

Dīdō Aenēam amat.

Dido loves Aeneas. — Dīdō nom. sg. · Aenēam acc. sg. · amat 3rd sg. Remember this one — the whole story is in it.

Servus dominum vocat.

The slave calls the master. — servus nom. sg. · dominum acc. sg. · vocat 3rd sg.

💡 COMING SOON · PREPOSITIONS

You'll sometimes see a phrase like in oppidō (“in the town”). That's the ablative case, formally taught in Module 08. For now, treat such a phrase as a fixed chunk and keep your focus on the subject and verb.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ DIDO AND AENEAS · AENEID, BOOKS 1–4

Storms sent by the goddess Juno drive Aeneas to Carthage, a new city rising on the coast of North Africa. Its queen, Dido, is everything Rome admired: capable, just, and tested by grief — she had already rebuilt her life after her brother murdered her husband. She understands loss.

Venus, Aeneas's divine mother, causes Dido to fall deeply in love with him. For a while Aeneas lingers — feasting, telling the story of Troy's fall, even helping raise the city's walls. But Jupiter sends the messenger Mercury with a reminder: Aeneas's fātum (fate) is Italy, not Carthage. A new Troy cannot be built on the shore of Africa.

Aeneas prepares to sail in secret. Dido discovers the plan and confronts him: only a monster, she says, could abandon someone who loved him. Aeneas answers that he does not go by his own will — Italiam nōn sponte sequor, “I do not seek Italy of my own accord.” He sails. Dido takes her own life. As the fleet pulls out to sea, Aeneas looks back and sees smoke rising from the city. He does not yet know what it means.

⚖️ PIETAS vs. AMOR

The Roman word for choosing the mission over the person is pietas — duty to the gods, to family, and to destiny. Rome honored Aeneas for it. But Virgil makes sure you also feel the cost. The whole tragedy is a collision between two Latin words you can already say: pietas (duty) and amor (love).

✅ TRY IT · the big word
Pietas is best described as:

In your own words: how does pietas explain Aeneas's choice to leave? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Pietas is dutiful devotion — to the gods, family, and destiny. Aeneas leaves not because he stops loving Dido, but because his pietas binds him to the fate of founding Rome. Duty wins over love, and Virgil wants us to feel how much that costs.
