Lesson 14.02
★ One Adjective, Three Genders — the 2-1-2 Pattern
Lesson · about 42 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned the rule. Today you get the pattern — and discover you already know every ending in it.
A 2-1-2 adjective is really three columns of endings you have already learned, bolted together. Its masculine forms are just 2nd-declension endings (like servus). Its feminine forms are 1st-declension endings (like puella). Its neuter forms are 2nd-declension neuter (like templum). That's the whole trick behind the name: 2–1–2. Learn to read the three columns, and you can make an adjective agree in any gender, number, and case.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read a dictionary entry like bonus, -a, -um and name its three gender forms.
2
I will be able to decline a 2-1-2 adjective across all five cases, singular and plural.
3
I will be able to choose the adjective form that agrees with a given noun.

📚 PART 2 · THREE FORMS IN THE DICTIONARY 8 min

Reading the entry

A 2-1-2 adjective appears in the dictionary with three endings, one per gender — for example bonus, bona, bonum (often shortened to bonus, -a, -um). Those three are just the nominative singular in masculine, feminine, and neuter:

GenderNom. sg. formBorrows endings from…
Masculinebonus2nd declension (like servus)
Femininebona1st declension (like puella)
Neuterbonum2nd declension neuter (like templum)

The stem is bon- — found by dropping the ending from the feminine form (bona → bon-). Build every other form on that stem. 2–1–2 is just a memory of which declension each gender copies.

Cover the words and look only at the endings: the masculine and neuter columns are your 2nd-declension endings; the feminine column is your 1st-declension endings. You are not learning new endings today — you are learning to pick the right column.

📌 PART 4 · AGREEMENT ACROSS DECLENSIONS 8 min

The adjective doesn't copy the noun's spelling

This is yesterday's twist, now visible in the chart. The adjective always uses its own 2-1-2 endings — it never simply repeats whatever the noun looks like. A 3rd-declension noun keeps its own endings; the adjective keeps its own; they still agree because gender, number, and case line up.

urbs antīqua

an ancient city — urbs is 3rd declension, feminine, nom. sg.; antīqua is the feminine nom. sg. of antīquus. Different endings, perfect agreement.

rēx magnus

a great king — rēx is 3rd declension, masculine, nom. sg.; magnus takes the masculine nom. sg. form.

Your turn: urbs (city) is feminine, 3rd declension. Put “the great city” into the accusative singular (its accusative is urbem). Say the whole phrase, then reveal.

urbem magnam — urbem is feminine accusative singular, so magnus takes its feminine accusative singular form magnam. The endings look nothing alike (-em vs. -am), yet they agree in gender, number, and case.
