⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP & READ ALONG 12 min
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Before you read: yesterday you met bonus (good) and malus (bad). If you wanted to attack someone in a speech, which would you reach for?

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. For every adjective, find its noun and check that the two agree in gender, number, and case. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself first.

Catilīna malus est.

Catiline is wicked. — Catilīna nom. sg. (masculine, despite the -a) · malus masc. nom. sg. adjective · est. Agreement by grammar, not by looks.

Cicerō cīvis bonus est.

Cicero is a good citizen. — Cicerō nom. sg. · cīvis nom. sg. (masculine) · bonus agrees, masc. nom. sg.

Catilīna multōs mīlitēs habet.

Catiline has many soldiers. — multōs masc. acc. pl. agrees with mīlitēs (acc. pl. of mīles) · habet 3rd sg.

Cicerō magnā vōce Catilīnam vocat.

Cicero calls Catiline in a loud voice. — magnā vōce abl. sg. (ablative of means, M08); magnā agrees with vōce (fem.) · Catilīnam acc. · vocat 3rd sg.

Rōma est urbs antīqua.

Rome is an ancient city. — urbs nom. sg. (3rd decl., feminine) · antīqua fem. nom. sg. Different declensions, still agreeing.

Cicerō malum Catilīnam vīcit.

Cicero defeated wicked Catiline. — malum masc. acc. sg. agrees with Catilīnam (acc.) · vīcit 3rd sg. perfect of vincō (M13).

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ INVECTIVE · CICERO vs. CATILINE, 63 BCE

In Module 11 you met encomium — the speech of praise. Invective (Latin vituperātiō) is its dark twin: the speech of attack and blame. Where praise builds a reputation, invective tears one down — and Rome's greatest master of both was Marcus Tullius Cicero.

In 63 bce, Cicero was consul — the highest office in the Republic. A ruined, ambitious aristocrat named Lucius Sergius Catiline was plotting armed revolution: to murder the consuls, cancel debts, and seize Rome. Cicero learned of the plot. Rather than arrest Catiline at once, he rose in the Senate — with Catiline himself sitting there — and delivered the First Catilinarian, the most famous attack speech in Latin literature.

It opens with hammer-blows:

“Quō ūsque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā?”
“How long, Catiline, will you go on abusing our patience?”
(Read this as a whole quotation — it uses forms beyond this course. But notice patientiā: it is the ancestor of English patience.)

Cicero did not just report facts. He painted Catiline — reckless, ruinous, malus — until the man's own allies fell silent. That night Catiline fled Rome to join his army. Cicero had defeated him with speech before a single sword was drawn.

⚖️ LANGUAGE AS A WEAPON

To describe is to judge. When Cicero fixed the adjective malus to Catiline, he was not adding detail — he was passing sentence. Words can wound as surely as weapons, and can defend a city without one. That power carries a duty: it can be used for the Republic or against it.

The weapon cuts both ways. Twenty years later, Cicero's own enemies had him killed and nailed his head and hands to the Rōstra — the very speaker's platform (Module 13) where his words had once ruled the Forum.

Now attack with an adjective. Cicero could turn the neutral Catilīna cīvis est (“Catiline is a citizen”) into a charge by adding the right form of malus. Build the accusing sentence, then reveal.

Catilīna cīvis malus est. — “Catiline is a bad citizen.” Cīvis is masculine nominative singular, so malus takes its masculine nominative singular form. One adjective, and a fact becomes an accusation — that is invective in miniature.
