Lesson 14.03
★ Eight Adjectives — and the English They Carry
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have the pattern. Today you get the words — and, as with the verbs, you half-know them already.
This module's eight adjectives are all 2-1-2, so yesterday's chart runs every one of them. And each is a root that English has grown words from. Learn magnus and you have explained magnificent, magnify, and magnate at a stroke. Today: meet the eight, and pull the English out of each. Two of them — bonus and malus — are the very words an orator reaches for to praise a friend or destroy an enemy.

For each adjective, cover the third column and try to name one English word before you peek. The ones you can generate yourself are the ones that will stick. Notice too how many come in opposites: magnus / parvus, bonus / malus, novus / antīquus.
📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

The vocabulary of blame

🌱 FROM MALUS (BAD, EVIL)

Malus is the root under a whole family of dark English words. Malevolent is mal- (bad) + velle (to wish) — “wishing harm.” Malice is the will to do evil. A malignant tumor is one that behaves badly; a malfunction is a machine working badly. When Cicero wanted to condemn Catiline, this is the register he drew on — the adjectives of blame. To describe someone as malus was to pass sentence on him in a single word.

This is why adjectives matter to a rhetorician: they carry judgment. Bonus and malus are not just vocabulary — they are verdicts. Tomorrow you will watch Cicero use them as weapons.
