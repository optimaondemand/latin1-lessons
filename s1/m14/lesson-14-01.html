Lesson 14.01
★ Words That Describe — and Judge
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Up to now your nouns have only named things. This week they start to be described — and, when Cicero gets hold of them, judged.
An adjective is a word that describes a noun: a great king, a wicked citizen, an ancient city. There is nothing to drill today — just one big idea to see clearly. In Latin an adjective can't be dropped in wherever you like: it has to agree with its noun in gender, number, and case. And here is the twist that trips everyone at first — agreement is a matter of grammar, not of matching letters by eye. Get that idea today, and the whole module falls into place.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 13 min

Adjectives must agree

In English, an adjective never changes: good boy, good girl, good towns — “good” sits there unmoved. Latin is different. A Latin adjective reshapes itself to match the noun it describes, lining up in three ways at once:

Match in…MeaningExample
Gendermasculine, feminine, or neutera feminine noun takes a feminine adjective
Numbersingular or plurala plural noun takes a plural adjective
Casenominative, accusative, genitive, dative, ablativean accusative noun takes an accusative adjective

So when a noun shifts case — as your nouns have been doing since Module 03 — the adjective riding along with it shifts too. Describe the good queen, the good queen's crown, a gift for the good queen: the word for “good” changes each time to keep pace.

The twist: agreement is grammar, not looks

Here is the point that saves you from the most common mistake. Agreement means matching gender, number, and case — it does not mean the two words rhyme or end in the same letters.

Looks can lie

nauta bonus
a good sailor

nauta ends in -a, but it is a masculine noun — so it takes the masculine form bonus. Not bona.

Grammar wins

poēta magnus
a great poet

poēta (poet) is masculine too, despite the -a. Its adjective is masculine magnus — matched by gender, not by ending.
💡 WHY THIS MATTERS THIS WEEK

Cicero's great enemy is named Catilīna — another masculine noun ending in -a. So when Cicero calls him wicked, the Latin is Catilīna malus, not Catilīna mala. Get agreement by grammar, and you can read the most famous attack speech in Roman history.

✅ TRY IT · English vs. Latin
English says “good boy, good girl, good towns” without changing “good.” In Latin the adjective…

Say it out loud: in Rēx bonus est (“The king is good”), point to the adjective and explain why its form is bonus. Then reveal.

The adjective is bonus. Rēx (king) is masculine, singular, and here nominative (it is the subject) — so the adjective takes the masculine singular nominative form bonus. Match the grammar, and the form follows.
