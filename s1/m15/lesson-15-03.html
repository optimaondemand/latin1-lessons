★ He, She, It — and the Ego Inside English
Lesson · about 39 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Latin has no dedicated word for “he.” It borrows a pointing word — and asks it to agree.
For “he, she, it, they,” Latin reaches for is, ea, id — a word that literally means “that one.” Because it stands in for a noun, it must match that noun in gender and number, exactly the way an adjective does. This week you learn only its nominative and accusative forms; the full set arrives next semester. Then we turn to a word you already carry into English every day: ego.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to give the nominative and accusative of is, ea, id and pick the form that agrees with a noun in gender and number.
2
I will be able to connect ego to English words such as ego, egotism, and egocentric.

📚 PART 2 · IS, EA, ID 13 min

One word, three genders

The pronoun is / ea / id means “he / she / it” (and, in the plural, “they”). It has a masculine, a feminine, and a neuter set, and you choose the one that agrees with the noun it replaces. Here are the two cases you need this semester:

CaseMasculineFeminineNeuter
Nom. sg.is — heea — sheid — it
Acc. sg.eum — himeam — herid — it
Nom. pl.eī — theyeae — theyea — they
Acc. pl.eōs — themeās — themea — them
⚠️ JUST THE NOM. AND ACC. FOR NOW

This word has more forms — a genitive, dative, and ablative in every gender, nine cells in all. You will learn the full paradigm in Latin 1, Semester 2. For now, master only the four rows above. That is plenty to read and write real sentences.

The key move is agreement: match the pronoun to the noun it points back to. Watch how the pronoun takes its cue from the earlier noun:

Rēx adest. Cicerō eum videt.

The king is here. Cicero sees him. — rēx is masculine, so “him” is eum (masc. acc.).

Rēgīna adest. Cicerō eam videt.

The queen is here. Cicero sees her. — rēgīna is feminine, so “her” is eam (fem. acc.).

Templum adest. Cicerō id videt.

The temple is here. Cicero sees it. — templum is neuter, so “it” is id (neut. acc.).

Note: adest (“is present / is here”) is a compound of esse; treat it as a fixed word for now.

📜 PART 3 · THE EGO IN ENGLISH 8 min

One tiny pronoun, a shelf of English

The Latin word for “I” walked straight into English unchanged. When you talk about someone's ego, you are literally saying the Latin for “I” — the self.

English wordMeaningHow ego is inside it
egothe self; one's sense of “I”the Latin word for “I,” borrowed whole
egotismconstantly talking about oneselfego (“I”) + -ism — the habit of “I, I, I”
egocentricself-centeredego (“I”) at the center
egomaniaobsessive self-importanceego (“I”) + mania (madness)
alter egoa “second self”Latin alter (other) + ego (I) — “another I”
🌱 A RHETORICIAN'S NOTE

An orator lives on the edge of ego. Used well, a bold “ego” commands the room — Cicero could stake his whole reputation on that one word. Used badly, the same word makes a speaker sound like an egotist. Same Latin root, two very different reputations.

✅ TRY IT · etymology
The English word egocentric means “self-centered.” So ego means:

Your turn. In “Rēgīna bona est. Cicerō ___ videt” (Cicero sees her), which pronoun fills the blank — and why? Say it, then reveal.

eam — the noun it points back to, rēgīna, is feminine singular, and here the pronoun is the object (accusative). Feminine + singular + accusative = eam. Agreement plus case does the work.
