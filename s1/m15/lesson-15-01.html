📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Here is a small surprise: Latin barely needs the word “I” — because the verb already says it.
When you write amō, the -ō ending already means “I”: amō is a complete sentence, “I love.” So why does Latin have a word for I at all? This week you meet the personal pronouns — ego, tū, nōs, vōs — and the answer turns out to be about power: a great orator like Cicero drops in ego not because he must, but to throw his whole weight onto that one word. Today, no forms to memorize — just one idea: in Latin, saying the subject out loud is a choice, and the choice is emphasis.

⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

The verb already knows

Think back to your verbs. The single word vocō means “I call” — the -ō ending carries the “I” all by itself. You never needed a separate word for the subject.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
If vocō already means “I call,” what do you suppose adding a separate word ego (“I”) in front of it could do?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Two jobs a pronoun does

A personal pronoun is a small word that stands in for a person: I, you, we, he, she, it, they. In Latin they do two different jobs, and it helps to keep them apart from the start.

As the subject — optional

Ego vocō.
I call.

The verb vocō already says “I.” Adding ego is a choice — it presses down on the “I.”

As the object — needed

Mē vocat.
He calls me.

Here the pronoun is the object. Nothing else can carry “me,” so the pronoun is genuinely required.

So the rule of thumb is simple. When the pronoun is the subject, Latin can leave it out — the verb ending already tells you who. When the pronoun is the object (“me, you, him, them”), you truly need it, because the verb ending only marks the doer, never the receiver.

💡 WHY THIS FITS THE RHETORIC UNIT

This quarter is about persuasion. When Cicero thundered ego at the Senate — “I saw it, I warned you” — the pronoun he didn't strictly need became a rhetorical hammer. Latin lets a speaker save the subject pronoun for exactly the moment he wants the room to feel it.
