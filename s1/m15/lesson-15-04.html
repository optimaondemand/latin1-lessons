⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 10 min

Pronouns in real sentences

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Before you read: think of one Latin word you already use in everyday English — maybe from a courtroom, a doctor's note, or a footnote. Which comes to mind first?

Now read each sentence aloud. Find the subject and the object, and notice how the pronoun form tells you which is which. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself first.

Ego sum Cicerō.

I am Cicero. — ego nom. (emphatic subject) · sum 1st sg. of esse · Cicerō predicate nom. (a name).

Catilīna mē timet.

Catiline fears me. — Catilīna nom. (subject) · mē acc. (object) · timet 3rd sg. of timēre.

Cicerō eōs monet.

Cicero warns them. — Cicerō nom. · eōs acc. masc. pl. (“them” — the conspirators) · monet 3rd sg.

Nōs Rōmam amāmus.

We love Rome. — nōs nom. (emphatic) · Rōmam acc. (object) · amāmus 1st pl.

💡 NOTICE THE EMPHASIS

In Ego sum Cicerō and Nōs Rōmam amāmus, the verb already carried the subject — so those pronouns are there for weight. That is precisely how an orator uses them: “I am Cicero,” “we love Rome.”

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 12 min
🏛️ LATIN, THE LANGUAGE OF EXPERTS

The Romans made Latin the language of law and public reason. Cicero — the greatest orator of the Republic — was also its most famous lawyer, and for centuries after Rome fell, the courts of Europe kept doing their serious business in Latin. That habit never fully died. Walk into a modern courtroom and you will still hear it.

A defense attorney files a writ of habeās corpus — literally “you shall have the body” — demanding that a detained person be brought before a judge. A trial may proceed in absentiā, “in absence,” when the accused will not appear. A judge may rule that an argument is flawed per sē — “in itself,” on its own terms.

Medicine and science kept Latin too. A prescription that reads per ōs means “by mouth.” A cell culture grown outside the body is grown in vītrō, “in glass.” None of this is decoration. Latin gives these fields a vocabulary that is exact, international, and old enough to feel authoritative — the very prestige Rome built through its orators, still working two thousand years later.

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

You already write Latin every day

🌱 “I.E.” = ID EST

The abbreviation i.e. that you use to say “that is” is short for the Latin id est — and look closely: id is the very pronoun you learned yesterday (“it / that”), and est is your old friend from esse (“is”). So id est literally means “it is” — “that is (to say).” Every time you type i.e., you are writing a two-word Latin sentence built from grammar you now own.

Its cousin e.g. is exemplī grātiā (“for the sake of example”), and etc. is et cētera (“and the rest”). The learned shorthand of English is quietly Latin.

This is the prestige of Latin in one small mark on the page. The language Cicero used to win cases still lets a modern writer sound precise in a single abbreviation.
