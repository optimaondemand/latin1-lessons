⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Before you read: when you hit an ablative phrase like in Italiā, what should you ask yourself first?

Now read each aloud. Find the subject and verb first, then handle the ablative phrase — ask whether it tells you where, with what, or from where. The gray line is your parse and translation; try it yourself before you read it.

Aenēas in Italiā habitat.

Aeneas now dwells in Italy. — Aenēas nom. sg. · in Italiā (in + abl., place where) · habitat 3rd sg.

Aenēas gladiō pugnat.

Aeneas fights with a sword. — gladiō abl. of means (the tool, no preposition) · pugnat 3rd sg.

Trōiānī gladiīs pugnant.

The Trojans fight with swords. — Trōiānī nom. pl. (the Trojans) · gladiīs abl. pl. of means · pugnant 3rd pl.

Latīnī in oppidō habitant.

The Latins dwell in the town. — Latīnī nom. pl. (the Latins) · in oppidō (in + abl.) · habitant 3rd pl.

Servus ex oppidō aquam portat.

The slave carries water out of the town. — servus nom. sg. · ex oppidō (ex + abl., from where) · aquam acc. sg. (object) · portat 3rd sg.

Nautae sine aquā labōrant.

The sailors toil without water. — nautae nom. pl. · sine aquā (sine + abl.) · labōrant 3rd pl.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ AENEAS IN ITALY · AENEID, BOOKS 7–8

After leaving the Underworld (Module 07), Aeneas and the Trojans at last make landfall in Italy, sailing up the river Tiber into Latium — the region that will one day cradle Rome. Its king, Latinus, rules an old Italian people called the Latins. An oracle has warned Latinus that his daughter Lavinia must marry a stranger from across the sea, not a local prince. When the Trojans arrive, Latinus reads the sign and welcomes them.

But Juno is not finished. She sends a Fury to inflame Queen Amata and the local prince Turnus, who had expected to marry Lavinia. A hunting accident — a tame stag shot by Aeneas's son — sets the countryside ablaze, and Latins and Trojans slide toward war.

Seeking allies, Aeneas travels further up the Tiber to a humble settlement called Pallanteum, ruled by the aged Greek king Evander. Evander walks Aeneas over the very hills where Rome will someday stand — the Palatine, the future Forum, the Capitoline — pointing out quiet pastures and springs that Virgil's Roman readers knew as the beating heart of the greatest city on earth. Aeneas is literally standing on the future of Rome. The destiny promised since the very first book is now under his feet.

⚖️ STANDING ON DESTINY · FĀTUM

Virgil wrote this scene for readers who lived in the finished Rome. When Evander shows Aeneas a muddy field, they knew it as the Forum; a wooded hill, and they knew it as the Capitol. That doubled vision — humble past, glorious future — is how the Romans felt their fātum (fate): not as luck, but as a destiny long promised and now being kept. Aeneas does not build Rome; he plants the seed that will become it.
