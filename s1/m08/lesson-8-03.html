📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Seven small words steer the ablative — and four of them are hiding inside English you use every day.
A preposition is a pointing word: it sits in front of a noun and tells you the relationship — in, with, out of, from, under, without. Latin has a group of these that always take the ablative. Today you meet all seven, add two new nouns (gladius and oppidum), and discover that ex, sub, cum, and ab have quietly seeded dozens of English words.

💡 ONE ABLATIVE NEEDS NO PREPOSITION

When the ablative names the tool or means of an action, Latin uses no preposition at all — the bare ablative is the “with.” So gladiō pugnat means “he fights with a sword.” This is the ablative of means, and you'll use it a lot this week.

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

Four prepositions, a whole shelf of English

🌱 PREPOSITIONS AT WORK IN ENGLISH

ex (out of) gives exit (the way out), external (on the outside), and exclude (to shut out). sub (under) gives submarine (under the sea) and subtract (to draw from beneath). cum (with) — often reshaped to com- or con- — gives cumulative (piling with more) and combine (to join with). ab (from, away) gives absent (being away) and abstract (drawn away from the concrete).

Prepositions are tiny, but they are the busiest word-builders in English. Learn these four Latin ones and you hold the key to hundreds of compounds — every time you see ex-, sub-, com-/con-, or ab- at the front of a word, a Roman preposition is at work.
