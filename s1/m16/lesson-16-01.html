📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A whole semester of Latin has let you state things. This week Latin learns to tell people what to do.
Rhetoric — this quarter's theme — is the art of moving an audience. And you cannot move people by describing the weather to them. You address them by name, and you tell them to act: Mīlitēs, pugnāte! — “Soldiers, fight!” Today you meet the two tools every Roman orator, general, and museum guide relied on: the imperative (the command form of a verb) and the vocative (the case for calling someone by name). No paradigm to drill today — just learn to recognize a command and a direct address the instant you see one.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 13 min

Two new tools: the command and the call

Up to now your verbs have been in the indicative mood — the mood of stating facts. This week adds the imperative mood — the mood of giving orders. And to say who you are ordering, Latin uses the vocative case — the case of direct address, of calling someone by name.

Stating — indicative

Mīles pugnat.
The soldier fights.

A report about someone. Nobody is being spoken to.

Commanding — imperative + vocative

Mīles, pugnā!
Soldier, fight!

The speaker calls the soldier (mīles, vocative) and orders him to act (pugnā, imperative).

Notice the two changes. pugnat (“he fights,” a report) became pugnā (“fight!”, an order) — that is the imperative. And mīles is no longer the subject of a sentence; it is the person being called — that is the vocative. You will build both forms properly over the next two days. Today, just feel the difference.

💡 YOU ALREADY DO THIS IN ENGLISH

“The dog runs” is a statement. “Run!” is a command — and notice English drops the subject entirely. Latin does the same: a command usually needs no separate word for “you,” because the imperative form already means “you, do this.”
