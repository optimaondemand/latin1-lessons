Lesson 3.03
★ Eight Nouns — and the English Hidden Inside Them
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have the pattern. Today you get the words — and discover you half-know them already.
This module's eight nouns are all first declension, so the four-form pattern from yesterday runs each of them. And here is the reward: English is full of their descendants. Learn aqua and you've explained aquatic, aquarium, and aqueduct in one stroke; learn via and you unlock viaduct, deviate, and even trivia. Today: meet the eight, and pull the English out of each.

📚 PART 2 · THE EIGHT NOUNS 15 min

Meet the family members

Each entry shows the nominative, then the genitive ending -ae (the standard dictionary tag that marks a noun as first declension), then its gender. All eight are first declension, so all eight take the four endings you learned yesterday. Note that most are feminine — but nauta is masculine: a sailor is a man, even though the word looks like the others.

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

One noun, a whole shelf of English

🌱 FROM AQUA (WATER)

Aqua gives English a whole watery family. Something aquatic lives in water; an aquarium is a “place for water” (and its creatures); aquamarine is the blue-green of “sea water” (aqua + mare). Best of all, an aqueduct is literally a “water-leader” (aqua + dūcere, to lead) — the stone channels the Romans built to carry fresh water across valleys into their cities. When you drink from a tap today, you're using Roman plumbing vocabulary.

The same holds for via (a Roman road): a viaduct carries a road across a valley, to deviate is to go “off the road” (dē + via), and trivia are the little things picked up where three roads meet (tri- + via). Each Latin noun is a root English has grown a dozen words from — so you're not learning eight words today, you're unlocking scores of English ones.

Now go the other way: viaduct and deviate come from via. What does via mean, and why does viaduct fit? Say it, then reveal.

“Road, way.” A viaduct carries a road (via) across a valley; to deviate is to stray “off the road” (dē + via). Latin roots turn English into a set of hints.
