⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the object (accusative), then check the verb. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it. Proper names (Aeneas, Juno, Venus, Sicily, Italy) and the verbs are glossed for you; the verbs come in Module 05.

Aenēās nauta est.

Aeneas is a sailor. — Aenēās nom. (subject) · nauta predicate nominative · est “is.”

Nautae aquam vident.

The sailors see the water. — nautae nom. pl. (subject) · aquam acc. sg. (object) · vident “they see” (glossed).

Nautae Siciliam vident.

The sailors see Sicily. — Siciliam acc. sg. (a first declension place-name, the object) · vident 3rd pl.

Iūnō nautās nōn amat.

Juno does not love the sailors. — Iūnō nom. (subject, glossed name) · nautās acc. pl. (object) · amat “loves” (glossed) · nōn “not.”

Venus nautās amat.

Venus loves the sailors. — Venus nom. (subject, glossed name) · nautās acc. pl. (object) · amat 3rd sg.

Aenēās Ītaliam amat.

Aeneas loves Italy. — Ītaliam acc. sg. (his fated land, the object) · amat 3rd sg.

💡 COMING SOON · ACTION VERBS

Words like videt/vident (see) and amat/amant (love) are action verbs. We're glossing them for now so you can read whole sentences — but you'll learn to build and conjugate them yourself in Module 05. For today, just trust the gloss and keep your focus on the noun endings.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ AENEAS LEAVES TROY · AENEID, BOOKS 1–3

Troy has fallen. After ten years of war the Greeks break the city open, and its towers burn through the night. Out of the ruins comes Aeneas, a Trojan prince, carrying his aged father on his shoulders and leading his young son by the hand. His mission is not revenge but survival: the gods have told him to find a new home in the west, in Italy, where his descendants will one day found Rome.

He gathers the Trojan survivors, builds a fleet, and sets out across the Mediterranean — the great sea the Romans called Mare Nostrum, “Our Sea.” The voyage is long and uncertain. Near the island of Sicily, a furious storm nearly destroys the ships, scattering the fleet and drowning some of his men. To a Roman reader, the sea itself was a test of endurance, and Aeneas passes it the way a Roman hero should — by holding his course.

But the storm is no accident. Behind it stands a quarrel among the gods — and that quarrel will shadow Aeneas across every mile of his journey.

⚖️ JUNO vs. VENUS

Venus, goddess of love, is Aeneas's own mother; she guards and guides him toward Italy. Juno, queen of the gods, hates the Trojans — she favors Carthage, Rome's future enemy, and knows that Aeneas's line is fated to destroy it. So Juno throws storms and delays in his path, while Venus works to protect him.

You can already say the heart of it in Latin: Venus nautās amat — “Venus loves the sailors” — while Iūnō nautās nōn amat — “Juno does not love the sailors.” The whole tug-of-war fits into two sentences you can read.

🌱 FROM TERRA (LAND)

Aeneas is searching for terra — a land to call home. That one noun gives English terrain (the lay of the land), territory (land under someone's control), and terrestrial (belonging to the earth — the opposite of extraterrestrial). When we call life on Earth “terrestrial,” we're using the very word Aeneas would have used for the ground he longed to reach.

Five checks — some test the story, some test your Latin reading. Take them slowly; the point is to prove you can both read the sentences and follow the tale.

✅ TRY IT · the setting
The Romans called the Mediterranean Mare Nostrum. What does that mean?

In your own words: how does the quarrel between Juno and Venus shape Aeneas's voyage? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Venus, his mother, guides and protects Aeneas toward Italy, while Juno — who favors Carthage and hates the Trojans — hurls storms and obstacles in his way (like the storm near Sicily). Aeneas's journey is caught between a goddess who loves the sailors and one who does not: Venus nautās amat; Iūnō nautās nōn amat.
