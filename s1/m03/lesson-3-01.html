★ One Noun, Many Jobs — Meet the Cases
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In Latin, a noun tells you its job by the way it ends — and today you learn to read that signal.
So far your nouns have done only one job: being the subject. This week they take on a second. When a Latin noun is the subject, it wears one ending; when it is the object of the action, it wears another. That change of ending is called a case. Today there is no full chart to memorize — just one idea to see clearly: in Latin the ending, not the word order, tells you who is doing what.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what a case is and why Latin nouns change their endings.
2
I will be able to tell the nominative (subject) job apart from the accusative (object) job.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

How does English tell you who did it?

Read these two English sentences. Same words — very different meanings.

The dog bites the man.

The dog is the biter; the man is bitten.

The man bites the dog.

Now the man is the biter. Only the word order changed.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
In English, what told you who did the biting?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

A case is a noun’s job

Back in Module 01 you met the nominative case — the form a noun takes when it is the subject, the one doing or being. This week you meet its partner, the accusative case — the form a noun takes when it is the direct object, the one receiving the action.

Nominative — the subject

nauta
the sailor (doing the action)

Ends in -a in the singular. This is the “who or what” of the sentence.

Accusative — the object

nautam
the sailor (receiving the action)

Ends in -am in the singular. This is the “whom or what” the action lands on.

Watch the same noun switch jobs. The verb videt means “sees” (you'll study verbs like it fully in Module 05 — for now just read it as “sees”):

Puella nautam videt.

The girl sees the sailor. — puella (-a) is the subject; nautam (-am) is the object.

Nauta puellam videt.

The sailor sees the girl. — now nauta is the subject and puellam is the object. The endings did the switching.

💡 THE INFLECTION PRINCIPLE, AGAIN

This is the very idea you met in Module 01: Latin marks a word's job on the word itself. English says who did what by position; Latin says it by ending. That freedom is why Latin can move its words around and still be perfectly clear.

✅ PART 3 · SPOT THE JOB 15 min

Subject or object?

You are not building full charts yet — that's tomorrow. Today just train your eye on the two singular signals: -a means subject, -am means object. Work through all five; wrong answers give a hint, and Reset lets you try again.

✅ TRY IT · find the subject
In Nauta aquam videt (“The sailor sees the water”), which word is the subject?
✅ TRY IT · read the ending
Which singular ending marks a noun as the direct object (accusative)?
✅ TRY IT · find the object
In Puella terram videt (“The girl sees the land”), which word is the object?
✅ TRY IT · name the case
The word via (a road) is doing the acting in its sentence. What case is it in?
✅ TRY IT · which is the object?
Which form would you use for “the girl” when she is the one being seen?

One to say out loud: in Nauta puellam videt, who is doing the seeing and who is being seen — and which ending told you? Say your reasoning, then reveal.

Nauta (ending -a) is the subject — the sailor does the seeing. Puellam (ending -am) is the object — the girl is seen. The endings, not the order, told you the jobs.
