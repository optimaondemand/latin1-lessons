📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned the idea. Today you learn the whole shape — four forms that run every -a noun in Latin.
A first declension noun has four forms in this module: subject and object, singular and plural. Learn the pattern on one word — puella, “girl” — and it transfers to nauta, terra, via, aqua, and every other -a noun. This is the single most useful table in the module, and you'll lean on it all week.

1
I will be able to produce the four forms of a first declension noun: -a, -am, -ae, -ās.
2
I will be able to match each ending to its case (nominative or accusative) and number (singular or plural).
3
I will be able to explain how the ending — not the word order — tells you the subject and the object.

📚 PART 2 · THE PARADIGM 7 min

The four forms of puella

Here is the model noun puella (girl) in the two cases you know, singular and plural. A full set of forms like this is called a declension — the noun-world's version of a verb's conjugation.

SingularPlural
Nominative (subject)puella — the girlpuellae — the girls
Accusative (object)puellam — the girlpuellās — the girls

The unchanging part — puell- — is the stem. Everything meaningful happens in the ending. And notice the plural object: puellās has a long ā. That macron is not decoration — it is how you tell the plural object apart from anything else by ear.

✅ PART 3 · LEARN THE ENDINGS 18 min

Four endings that work on every -a noun

Strip puella down to its endings and you have the master set. Attach these to any first declension stem and you can make its subject and object forms:

EndingCase & numberJob
-anominative singularone subject
-amaccusative singularone object
-aenominative pluralmore than one subject
-āsaccusative pluralmore than one object
💡 ONE FORM TO WATCH

The ending -ae (nominative plural) and the singular object -am look a little alike at a glance — but they do opposite jobs. -am = one object; -ae = more than one subject. When you read, check the last letters carefully.

Now put the pattern to work — five reps, building forms and reading endings both directions. Keep the chart above in view; the goal is to make the four endings automatic.

Now produce a full set: give all four forms of via (road) — nominative and accusative, singular and plural. Say them aloud, then reveal to check.

Nominative: via (sg.), viae (pl.).  Accusative: viam (sg.), viās (pl.). Same four endings — -a, -am, -ae, -ās — on the stem vi-.
📌 PART 4 · A LATIN SUPERPOWER 8 min

The ending outranks the word order

Because the ending carries the job, a Latin writer can move the words for emphasis or style and the meaning holds. English cannot do this. Watch — both sentences mean the same thing (videt = “sees,” glossed until Module 05):

Nauta terram videt.

The sailor sees the land. — normal order: subject, object, verb.

Terram nauta videt.

Still “the sailor sees the land.” Terram moved to the front for emphasis, but its -am keeps it the object — and nauta keeps its -a, so the sailor is still the one seeing.

In English, moving “the land” to the front would change who sees whom. In Latin the endings hold the meaning in place, so word order becomes a tool for style rather than a rule you must obey. Still, the everyday default order you'll write is Subject–Object–Verb.

Your turn: what does Aquam puella videt mean, and how do the endings tell you? Say it, then reveal.

“The girl sees the water.” Aquam (-am) is the object even though it comes first; puella (-a) is the subject. Endings, not position, decide.
