★ Reading Latin — The Three Duties of the Orator

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Endings plus nouns equals reading. Today the pieces click together — around a Roman question: what makes a truly great speaker?
First you read real Latin sentences built from this week's third-declension nouns and the verbs you already own. Then you meet Cicero's Dē Ōrātōre — his great dialogue on the art of speaking — and its famous claim that the orator has three duties: docēre (to teach), dēlectāre (to delight), and movēre (to move). No new grammar today: today you use what you built.

⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Before you read: when you picture a great orator in front of a crowd, what do you imagine they're mostly trying to do?

Now read each sentence aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the verb, then any object (accusative), and watch for a genitive (“of”). The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it.

Rēx urbem tenet.

The king holds the city. — rēx nom. sg. (subject) · urbem acc. sg. (object) · tenet 3rd sg.

Mīlitēs hostēs vincunt.

The soldiers conquer the enemies. — mīlitēs nom. pl. · hostēs acc. pl. · vincunt 3rd pl.

Cīvēs vōcem rēgis amant.

The citizens love the king's voice. — cīvēs nom. pl. · vōcem acc. sg. (object) · rēgis gen. sg. (“of the king”) · amant 3rd pl.

Māter fābulam narrāvit.

The mother told a story. — māter nom. sg. · fābulam acc. sg. · narrāvit 3rd sg. perfect (“told”).

Ōrātor docet, dēlectat, movet.

The orator teaches, delights, and moves. — ōrātor nom. sg. (a 3rd-decl. noun, ōrātor, ōrātōris, “speaker”) · three present-tense verbs. Remember this one — it is today's culture reading in miniature.

💡 NOTICE THE GENITIVE

In vōcem rēgis, two third-declension nouns sit side by side: vōcem (accusative, the object) and rēgis (genitive, “of the king”). The endings do all the work — -em marks the object, -is marks the “of.”

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ CICERO'S DĒ ŌRĀTŌRE · 55 BCE

After a lifetime in the Forum, Cicero — Rome's greatest orator — wrote Dē Ōrātōre (“On the Orator”), a dialogue in which senior statesmen sit at a country villa and debate what makes a great speaker. His answer is bracing: real eloquence is not a bag of tricks. The ideal orator must be widely learned — in history, law, philosophy, and human character — and must be a person of judgment and integrity. Technique alone produces a clever noise; wisdom produces a voice worth hearing.

From this comes Cicero's most quoted idea: the orator has three officia (duties). He must docēre — teach the audience, making the facts clear. He must dēlectāre — delight them, holding their attention with grace and wit. And he must movēre — move them, stirring the emotions that make people decide and act.

A speech that only teaches is dry; one that only delights is empty entertainment. The greatest oratory does all three — and Cicero prized movēre most, because in the end persuasion is not just understanding, but caring enough to act. This is the standard against which every Roman speech in this quarter was measured.

⚖️ DOCĒRE · DĒLECTĀRE · MOVĒRE

Notice that Cicero's three duties are three verbs — speech is something you do to and for other people. To teach respects your listeners' minds; to delight respects their attention; to move respects their freedom to choose. Good rhetoric, for Cicero, is not manipulation but service: it gives an audience what it needs to judge well. That is why the same word, vōx — a “voice” — can mean both a sound and a person's public stand.

✅ TRY IT · the key word
Cicero prized movēre most of the three duties. It means:

In your own words: why did Cicero think the greatest orator must do more than just docēre (teach)? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Teaching alone reaches only the mind: the audience understands but may not care. Cicero believed persuasion also needs dēlectāre (to hold attention) and above all movēre (to stir feeling), because people act on what moves them, not merely on what they know. A great orator serves all three at once — clear, engaging, and moving.
