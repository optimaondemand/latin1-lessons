📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday the genitive told you which family. Today it hands you the stem — and the stem builds every case.
Here's the reward for all the strange nominatives: the third declension has one set of endings, and they work the same whether the noun is a king, a mother, or a voice. Find the stem (drop -is from the genitive), then add the endings. Today you learn the full pattern — nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, singular and plural — plus the one small twist for neuter nouns.

📚 PART 2 · FIND THE STEM 7 min

Step one: get the stem

To decline any third-declension noun, start from the genitive singular and drop the -is. What's left is the stem — the true body of the word that every case is built on.

Genitive singularDrop -isStem
rēgisrēgisrēg-
mīlitismīlitismīlit-
patrispatrispatr-

Notice how the stem often looks nothing like the nominative: rēx hides rēg-, and pater quietly drops its e to become patr-. This is exactly why yesterday's rule matters — the genitive, not the nominative, is where you find the real stem.

✅ PART 3 · ADD THE ENDINGS 18 min

Step two: the case endings

Add the endings to the stem. Here is the model noun rēx, rēgis (king), fully declined. The nominative singular is the one form you simply learn from the dictionary; every other form is stem + ending.

CaseSingularPlural
Nominativerēx — a king (subject)rēgēs — kings
Genitiverēgis — of a kingrēgum — of kings
Dativerēgī — to/for a kingrēgibus — to/for kings
Accusativerēgem — a king (object)rēgēs — kings
Ablativerēge — by/with a kingrēgibus — by/with kings

Strip out the stem and the endings stand on their own. Learn these once and they run every consonant-stem noun in the language:

💡 THE THREE TO KNOW COLD

If you master just three endings this week, make them -is (genitive singular, “of”), -em (accusative singular, the object), and -ēs (nominative or accusative plural). These three do most of the work in real sentences.

Now put the pattern to work — five reps, building forms and reading endings both directions. Keep the charts in view; the goal is to make the endings automatic.

📌 PART 4 · THE NEUTER TWIST 9 min

One rule for neuter nouns

Some third-declension nouns are neuter, and they follow the same endings with just two changes you already know from neuter nouns in the 2nd declension:

Rule 1 — nom. = acc.

The nominative and accusative are always identical, singular and plural. Context tells you which job the word is doing.

Rule 2 — plural in -a

The nominative/accusative plural ends in -a, not -ēs. Everything else matches the chart above.

Take nōmen, nōminis (name, neuter). Stem nōmin-. Its accusative singular is not nōminem — it stays nōmen. Its plural “names” is nōmina.

Your turn: for the neuter noun nōmen, nōminis, what is the accusative singular, and why? Say it, then reveal.

nōmen — unchanged. Because nōmen is neuter, its nominative and accusative are identical, so the object form matches the subject form. (A masculine noun like rēx would take -em → rēgem, but neuters never do.) The plural “names” would be nōmina.
