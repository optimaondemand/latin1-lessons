★ Ten Nouns — and the English Hidden Inside Them
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have the pattern. Today you get the words — and meet one small sub-family with a single extra habit.
This module's ten nouns are all third declension, so the endings from yesterday run every one of them. But the third declension has a twin sub-group called i-stems — nouns like cīvis (citizen) and urbs (city) — that behave identically except for one tell: their genitive plural ends in -ium, not -um. And because third-declension nouns are so ancient and so common, an enormous share of English descends from them. Learn rēx and you've explained regal, reign, and regent at once.

Group two: i-stems

These five are i-stems. They decline just like consonant stems in the cases you'll use most — but their genitive plural ends in -ium. Two visible clues that a noun is an i-stem: the nominative and genitive singular match (cīvis, cīvis), or the nominative ends in -s right after a consonant (urbs, mōns).

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

One noun, a whole civic vocabulary

🌱 FROM CĪVIS (CITIZEN)

Cīvis is the root of nearly every word for organized public life. Civil means “of the citizens”; civilization is what citizens build together; a citizen is literally one who belongs to the cīvitās (the community of cīvēs). For a Roman, being a cīvis Rōmānus — a Roman citizen — carried rights and dignity that oratory in the Forum was meant to defend. When Cicero spoke, he spoke to and for the cīvēs.

This is why third-declension vocabulary pays compound interest: each noun is a root English has grown a cluster of words from. You're not memorizing ten words today — you're unlocking scores of English ones.
