Lesson 12.01
★ The Third Declension — Latin's Biggest Family of Nouns
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two declensions gave you a comfort you're about to lose — and one small key that gives it right back.
With the 1st and 2nd declensions, the dictionary form told you almost everything: -a meant first, -us or -um meant second. The third declension is different — and it's the largest group of nouns in all of Latin. Its nominative comes in every imaginable shape: rēx, mīles, pater, vōx, urbs. There's no single “look” to memorize. Today there is nothing to drill — just one liberating idea: you don't identify a third-declension noun by its nominative at all. You identify it by its genitive, and that genitive always ends the same way.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why the third declension has no single nominative pattern.
2
I will be able to recognize a third-declension noun by its genitive singular ending -is.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

Look at the dictionary entry

Every Latin noun is listed with two forms: the nominative, then the genitive. You already know these first two:

nauta, nautae

sailor — 1st declension (genitive in -ae)

servus, servī

slave — 2nd declension (genitive in -ī)

Now here is a new one: rēx, rēgis (king).

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Looking at rēx, rēgis, what jumps out at you compared to nauta, nautae?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Why the nominative can't be trusted

In the first two declensions, the nominative wore a uniform. Third-declension nouns show up in every kind of clothing:

Familiar — uniform nominatives

nauta (1st)
servus (2nd)
templum (2nd)

The ending tells you the declension at a glance.

Third — every shape

rēx (king)
mīles (soldier)
pater (father)
vōx (voice)
urbs (city)

No two look alike. The nominative is no help.

So the Romans give you a second address for every noun: the genitive singular. And for the third declension, that genitive is wonderfully consistent — it always ends in -is.

Dictionary entryGenitive singularDeclension
rēx, rēgisrēgisthird
mīles, mīlitismīlitisthird
vōx, vōcisvōcisthird
💡 WHY THIS MATTERS

Don't panic at the strange nominatives. Whenever you meet a new noun whose genitive ends in -is, you instantly know two things: it's third declension, and it will take the third-declension endings you'll learn tomorrow. One ending — -is — unlocks the whole family.
