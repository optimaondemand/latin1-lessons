★ Reading Latin — A City Rising from the Shore

Endings plus nouns equals reading. Today the pieces click together — and a great city rises out of the sea.
First you read real Latin sentences that mix first and second declension nouns — masters and slaves, boys and friends, temples and towns. Then you sail with Aeneas into Carthage: the greatest city of the western Mediterranean, built by Dido, a Phoenician queen, on the coast of Africa. ⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 4 min

Before you read

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer

📚 PART 2 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the object (accusative), and check the noun endings against this week's paradigm. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it. The action verbs are glossed; focus on the nouns.

Servus dominum videt.

The slave sees the master. — servus nom. sg. (subject) · dominum acc. sg. (object) · videt = “sees” (verb glossed until M05)

Dominus servōs vocat.

The master calls the slaves. — dominus nom. sg. · servōs acc. pl. (2nd decl. masc.) · vocat = “calls.”

Puer amīcum amat.

The boy loves his friend. — puer nom. sg. (irregular: no -us) · amīcum acc. sg. · amat = “loves.”

Nautae oppidum spectant.

The sailors gaze at the town. — nautae nom. pl. (1st decl.) · oppidum acc. sg. neuter (nom. = acc.) · spectant = “they gaze at.”

Carthāgō magnum oppidum est.

Carthage is a great town. — Carthāgō (proper noun, treat as nom.) · oppidum here is a predicate after est, so it is nominative · magnum = “great” (neuter, agreeing with oppidum) · est = “is.”

💡 A NEUTER READING TIP

Because a neuter like oppidum looks the same as subject and object, let the other noun and the word order guide you. In Nautae oppidum spectant, the plural verb spectant matches the plural nautae, so nautae must be the subject and oppidum the object.

📚 PART 3 · CULTURAL READING 12 min
🏛️ CARTHAGE · THE GREAT CITY OF THE WEST

Long before Rome ruled the sea, the greatest city of the western Mediterranean was Carthage (Carthāgō). Its name comes from the Phoenician Qart-ḥadašt — “New City.” It was founded by colonists from Tyre, a Phoenician port on the coast of what is now Lebanon, and by tradition its founder was a queen: Dido (also called Elissa), who fled her homeland after her brother murdered her husband and led her people across the sea to build a new home on the shore of North Africa.

Carthage grew rich as a hub of trade. Phoenician ships carried purple dye, silver, tin, and grain across the whole Mediterranean, and the city's double harbor could hold hundreds of vessels. Behind mighty walls stood templa (temples), markets, and shipyards — everything a Latin speaker packed into the small word oppidum.

In Virgil's Aeneid, storms drive Aeneas to this very shore. He arrives as Dido's workers are still raising the walls, and the queen welcomes the shipwrecked Trojans warmly. For a moment two founders stand face to face — one building Carthage, the other fated to found Rome. What passes between them is one of the most famous stories in all of Latin literature, and you will read it in full next module.

🌱 TWO ROOTS FROM THE STORY

fīlius (son) → filial, affiliate. Aeneas carries his young fīlius, Iulus, out of burning Troy; centuries later the family of Julius Caesar claimed descent from that same boy. A filial duty is a son's duty; to affiliate is to be joined as if into a family.

bellum (war) → bellicose, belligerent, antebellum. Rome and Carthage would one day fight three terrible wars — the Punic Wars. From bellum English gets belligerent (“war-waging”), bellicose (“eager to fight”), and antebellum (“before the war”).

In your own words: what did the small Latin word oppidum really contain for the people of the ancient world? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

An oppidum was far more than houses. Behind its walls lay safety from raiders, harbors and markets for trade, templa for worship, and a shared identity and law. Carthage shows all of it at once — which is why founding a city was considered one of the greatest things a leader like Dido (or Aeneas) could do.
