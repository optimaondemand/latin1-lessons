★ Two Shapes, One Family — Masculine and Neuter
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned to spot the family. Today you learn how it changes shape.
The second declension comes in two flavors: masculine nouns that end their nominative in -us (like servus), and neuter nouns that end in -um (like templum). They share almost all their endings — and the neuter has one beautiful shortcut you will love. Today you learn both paradigms: subject and object, singular and plural.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to give the nominative and accusative, singular and plural, of a 2nd declension masculine noun (-us / -um / -ī / -ōs).
2
I will be able to do the same for a 2nd declension neuter noun (-um / -um / -a / -a).
3
I will be able to explain the neuter rule: nominative and accusative are always identical.

📚 PART 2 · THE MASCULINE PARADIGM 12 min

Masculine: servus (slave)

You only need two cases this module — nominative (the subject) and accusative (the direct object) — in both numbers. Here is the model masculine noun servus, fully laid out:

SingularPlural
Nominative (subject)servus — the slaveservī — the slaves
Accusative (object)servum — the slaveservōs — the slaves
💡 FOUR ENDINGS TO KNOW

Nom. sg. -us · acc. sg. -um · nom. pl. -ī · acc. pl. -ōs. Find the stem the same way you find a declension: drop the genitive ending. servus, servī → stem serv-, then add the four endings.

The same four endings run every masculine noun in the family:

amīcus (friend)dominus (master)fīlius (son)
Nom. sg.amīcusdominusfīlius
Acc. sg.amīcumdominumfīlium
Nom. pl.amīcīdominīfīliī
Acc. pl.amīcōsdominōsfīliōs
⭐ ONE FRIENDLY EXCEPTION · puer

A few masculine nouns show no -us in the nominative: puer (boy) is simply puer. But its genitive is puerī, so it is still second declension, and every other form is regular: acc. sg. puerum, nom. pl. puerī, acc. pl. puerōs. Learn it from its dictionary entry: puer, puerī.

📌 PART 3 · THE NEUTER PARADIGM 10 min

Neuter: templum (temple)

The neuter nouns end their nominative singular in -um and share the family stem, but they follow one rule that makes them easier, not harder:

SingularPlural
Nominative (subject)templum — the templetempla — the temples
Accusative (object)templum — the templetempla — the temples
🌟 THE NEUTER RULE

In every neuter noun, in every declension, the nominative and the accusative are identical. Singular: both templum. Plural: both templa. And notice the neuter plural always ends in -a — do not mistake it for a 1st declension singular like puella! The dictionary entry (templum, templī) keeps you straight.

So oppidum (town) and bellum (war) work the same way: oppidum / oppida, bellum / bella. Because nom. and acc. look alike, you tell a neuter's job from the rest of the sentence — usually word order and the other noun's ending.
