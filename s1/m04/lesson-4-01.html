📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have spent three modules with one family of nouns. This week you meet a second — and learn how to tell them apart at a glance.
Every noun you have used so far — nauta, puella, terra, via — belongs to the first declension. But Latin sorts its nouns into families, and most of the words you will need to follow Aeneas across the sea live in a different one: the second declension. Today there is nothing to drill and no paradigm to memorize — just one skill worth its weight in gold: reading a dictionary entry so that the noun tells you which family it comes from.

I will be able to explain what a declension is and why Latin keeps more than one.
2

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Latin sorts its nouns into families

Latin has five noun families, called declensions. Every declension has its own set of endings — but the jobs those endings do are the same in all of them. A nominative is always a subject; an accusative is always a direct object — no matter which family the noun comes from. You learned that machinery in the first declension. This week you simply install a second set of endings alongside it.

First declension — you know this

nauta (subject)
nautam (object)

Mostly feminine. Nom. sg. in -a, genitive in -ae.

Second declension — new this week

servus (subject)
servum (object)

Mostly masculine and neuter. Genitive in -ī.

So how do you know, when you meet a new noun, which family it belongs to? You cannot always tell from the nominative alone — but the genitive singular never lies. That is why dictionaries always list it second.

💡 THE DICTIONARY ENTRY IS A MAP

A Latin noun is listed as two forms: servus, servī. The first is the nominative singular; the second is the genitive singular. Read the genitive ending: -ae means first declension, -ī means second. You do not need to translate the genitive yet — just use it as a family label.
