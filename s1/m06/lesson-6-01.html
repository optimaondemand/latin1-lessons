Lesson 6.01
★ The Case of “Of” — How Latin Shows Belonging
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
English needs an extra word — of — or a little apostrophe to show belonging. Latin needs neither.
Say “the queen's crown” or “the crown of the queen” and English has to add something. Latin does the same job by changing one ending — and that ending has a name you already know: the genitive. There is nothing to drill today. Just one idea: in Latin, belonging lives inside the word. This week you will learn to build it, read it, and hear the gods of Aeneas argue over whose rēgnum the world will be.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what the genitive case does — mark one noun as belonging to, or “of,” another.
2
I will be able to recognize that Latin shows “of” and possession by changing a noun's ending, not by adding a word.
⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

A puzzle before we start

So far your Latin nouns have done two jobs: they act (nominative, the subject) and they get acted upon (accusative, the object). But no case yet lets one noun simply belong to another.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
English shows “the queen's crown” two different ways. Before we look at Latin — how do you think a language could show belonging without a word like “of”?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Belonging lives in the ending

English has two tools for showing that one thing belongs to another: the word “of” and the apostrophe-s. Latin has one tool for both: the genitive case. Watch how the extra English machinery collapses into a single changed ending.

English — add machinery

the crown of the queen
the queen's crown

Two separate ways, both needing an added word or mark.

Latin — change the ending

corōna rēgīnae
the crown of the queen / the queen's crown

One form, rēgīnae, does the whole job.

The noun that owns or describes — here, the queen — goes into the genitive. The thing owned keeps whatever case its own job in the sentence requires. So in corōna rēgīnae, corōna can still be a subject or object, while rēgīnae quietly says “of the queen.”

💡 THE QUESTION THE GENITIVE ANSWERS

When you meet a genitive, ask whose? or of what? The answer is the genitive noun. “Whose crown? The queen's.” “The anger of whom? Of the gods.” The case even gets its name from this idea — it comes from Latin genus, generis, “origin, family” (more on that Thursday).

“Sailor” goes genitive — it is the owner. It answers whose ship? the sailor's. In Latin that would be nāvis nautae — literally “ship of-the-sailor.” The owner takes the genitive; the thing owned keeps its own case.
