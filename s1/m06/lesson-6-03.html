Lesson 6.03
★ Words of the Gods — and the English Inside Them
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have the endings. Today you get the words — eight of them, all from the world of Roman religion.
These eight nouns name the gods, gifts, and forces that steer the story of Aeneas: deus and dea, dōnum and rēgnum, caelum, glōria, īra, and Neptūnus. And, as always with Latin, learning them unlocks a shelf of English words you already use — deity, donate, celestial, glory, ire. Today: meet the eight, and pull the English out of each.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to give the meaning, gender, and declension of the eight new vocabulary words.
2
I will be able to connect each Latin word to at least one English word that descends from it.

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 6 min

One gift, a whole family of English

🌱 FROM DŌNUM (GIFT) AND DŌNĀRE (TO GIVE)

Dōnum is a gift — and in Roman religion, the gift you place on an altar for a god. From it English draws donate, donor, and donation. Even the word pardon hides it: per- (thoroughly) + dōnāre (to give) — to “give completely,” to forgive a debt. When you donate today, you are doing what a Roman did when laying a dōnum before an altar two thousand years ago.

This is why Latin vocabulary pays compound interest: each word is a root English has grown a dozen words from. You are not memorizing eight words today — you are unlocking scores of English ones.

Now go the other way: glōria gives English glory and glorify. What does glōria mean, and what famous Latin motto uses it? Say it, then reveal.

“Glory, fame, renown.” The old motto Sīc trānsit glōria mundī means “thus passes the glory of the world” — a reminder that even the greatest fame fades. Notice glōria and mundī: mundī is a genitive, “of the world.”
