💡 HOW TO BUILD ONE

Take the base of the noun (the part before the nominative ending) and add the genitive ending. rēgīna → base rēgīn- → gen. sg. rēgīnae, gen. pl. rēgīnārum. dominus → base domin- → gen. sg. dominī, gen. pl. dominōrum.

📌 PART 3 · NEUTERS & THE TRAP 8 min

Two things worth knowing

Good news about neuters. Some second-declension nouns are neuter (dōnum gift, rēgnum kingdom, caelum sky). Their nominative and accusative behave differently — but their genitive is completely regular, exactly like servus: gen. sg. -ī, gen. pl. -ōrum.

dōnum → dōnī (of the gift) → dōnōrum (of the gifts)

Neuter, but the genitive follows the ordinary 2nd-declension pattern — nothing special to learn.

⚠️ THE TRAP · GEN. SG. LOOKS LIKE NOM. PL.

Look carefully: 1st-declension -ae is both the genitive singular and the nominative plural. So puellae can mean “of the girl” (gen. sg.) or “the girls” (nom. pl.). The same overlap hits 2nd declension: -ī is genitive singular and nominative plural, so servī is either “of the slave” or “the slaves.” This is normal Latin. The sentence around the word — word order and the verb — tells you which it is.

Don't let the trap alarm you. If a word like rēgīnae sits right beside another noun, it is almost certainly the genitive (“of the queen”). If it stands as the subject with a plural verb, it is the nominative plural (“the queens”).
