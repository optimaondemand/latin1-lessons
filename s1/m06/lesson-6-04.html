Endings plus words equals reading. Today the genitive carries meaning — and the gods take the stage.
First you read real phrases and sentences that use the genitive and this week's vocabulary. Then you meet the three gods who shape Aeneas's story — Venus his mother, Jupiter who guarantees his fate, and Juno whose īra hunts him across the sea — and the Roman idea of fātum: a destiny even the gods must bow to. No new grammar today: today you use what you built.

⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Genitives you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the genitive noun, name what it belongs to, and translate with “of” or apostrophe-s. The gray line is your parse — try it yourself before you read it.

fīlia rēgīnae

the daughter of the queen / the queen's daughter — rēgīnae gen. sg. modifies fīlia

īra deōrum

the anger of the gods / the gods' anger — deōrum gen. pl. modifies īra

Aenēas fīlius deae est.

Aeneas is the son of a goddess. — fīlius nom. (predicate) · deae gen. sg. · est (is). The goddess is Venus.

Nautae dōna deōrum portant.

The sailors carry the gifts of the gods. — nautae nom. pl. · dōna acc. pl. (neuter) · deōrum gen. pl. · portant 3rd pl.

Nauta glōriam Rōmae cantat.

The sailor sings the glory of Rome. — nauta nom. sg. · glōriam acc. sg. · Rōmae gen. sg. · cantat 3rd sg.

💡 WATCH THE PAIR

A genitive almost always sits right beside the noun it belongs to. In īra deōrum, the pair is easy to see: deōrum (“of the gods”) leans on īra (“anger”). When you spot a genitive, look at its neighbor first.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 15 min
🏛️ THE GODS BEHIND THE JOURNEY · AENEID, BOOKS 1–4

Aeneas never travels alone. Above the human story, the gods are always at work — helping, hindering, arguing over him. Three matter most. Their names appear below; two of them belong to the third declension, which you have not yet learned, so their genitive forms are glossed for you.

GodLatin (nom., gen.)Role in the story
VenusVenus, Veneris (f.) — 3rd decl., glossedAeneas's mother; protects and guides him; pleads his case before Jupiter
JupiterIuppiter, Iovis (m.) — 3rd decl., glossedKing of the gods; guarantees the fātum that Aeneas will found the Roman race
JunoIūnō, Iūnōnis (f.) — 3rd decl., glossedAeneas's divine enemy; her īra sends the storms and delays

Notice how the story is built out of the very word you are learning. Aeneas is fīlius Veneris — “the son of Venus.” He is driven by the īra Iūnōnis — “the wrath of Juno.” The genitive is doing the storytelling.

🌬️ ĪRA IŪNŌNIS — THE WRATH OF JUNO

Virgil opens the whole Aeneid by asking why a goddess would hound a good man so relentlessly: tantaene animīs caelestibus īrae? — “Can there be such anger in the minds of the gods?” Juno resents the Trojans for old insults and fears a prophecy that their descendants will one day destroy her beloved Carthage. The genitive phrase īra Iūnōnis is, in a sense, the engine of the whole poem — every storm, delay, and disaster flows from it.

⚖️ FĀTUM vs. DIVINE WILL

Here is the deep Roman idea: even the gods are not fully in charge. Juno can delay Aeneas with her īra, and Venus can help him — but neither can change his fātum. Fate decrees that Aeneas will reach Italy and father the Roman race, and Jupiter's job is to guarantee that destiny, not invent it. Divine will bends the path; fātum fixes the destination. That tension — gods who quarrel beneath a fate none can break — runs through the entire poem.

In your own words: why can the phrase īra Iūnōnis be called “the engine of the Aeneid”? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Īra Iūnōnis means “the wrath of Juno.” Her anger at the Trojans is what sets the storms, delays, and disasters in motion — the whole plot of Aeneas's wandering flows from it. Fate still fixes where he ends up, but Juno's īra makes the journey long and costly. It is the force that gets the story moving.
