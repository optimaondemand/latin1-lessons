⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences in the past

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
When you read a verb ending in -vit or -ērunt, what should your first instinct be?

Read each aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the verb, and let the perfect ending tell you the action is done. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself first.

Cicerō Pompeium laudāvit.

Cicero praised Pompey. — Cicerō nom. sg. (subject) · Pompeium acc. sg. (object) · laudāvit 3rd sg. perfect.

Pompeius glōriam habuit.

Pompey had glory. — Pompeius nom. sg. · glōriam acc. sg. · habuit 3rd sg. perfect of habēre.

Nautae Pompeium laudāvērunt.

The sailors praised Pompey. — nautae nom. pl. · Pompeium acc. sg. · laudāvērunt 3rd pl. perfect.

Cicerō in oppidō fuit.

Cicero was in the town. — Cicerō nom. sg. · in oppidō abl. (place where) · fuit 3rd sg. perfect of esse — the irregular fu- root.

💡 ESSE IN THE PERFECT

You've used est and sunt in the present. In the perfect, esse is irregular: it builds on the root fu- — fuī, fuistī, fuit, fuimus, fuistis, fuērunt (“I was, you were, he/she/it was…”). The endings are the same six you already know; only the root is unusual.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ THE ENCOMIUM · CICERO'S PRO LĒGE MĀNILIĀ (66 BCE)

An encomium (from the Greek enkōmion) is a formal speech of praise — a public honoring of a person's virtues and deeds. To Romans this was serious business, not empty flattery. Public praise granted a man glōria and lasting fame, and in a city with no newspapers, a great speech in the Forum could shape a whole career.

In 66 BCE a young senator named Cicero rose before the Roman people to support a law that would give the general Pompey command of the war against King Mithridates in the East. It was Cicero's first political speech to the people, and much of it is a sustained encomium of Pompey. Cicero praises his courage, his self-control, his authority, and his remarkable good fortune — painting Pompey as the one man equal to the task.

The speech worked: the law passed, and Pompey got his command. But look closely at what happened — Cicero built a man's power out of words. That is the quarter's whole theme: in Rome, the ability to praise (and to persuade) was a form of power. To be praised well in the Forum was to be given something that outlived you.

⚖️ HONOR & HONEST PRAISE

Praise carries a duty. An encomium is meant to honor what is genuinely good — real courage, real service — not to flatter the powerful for advantage. The Romans drew a hard line between laus (deserved praise) and mere flattery. When you praise truly, you tell the truth about what a person did — which is exactly why praise lives in the perfect tense: it points to completed deeds that actually happened.

🌱 ROOTS OF RECOGNITION

The Latin of praise is still all around you. Laudāre lives in laud, laudatory, and applaud; glōria in glory and glorify; honor in honorable and honorary. Even perfectus — “carried through” — gives us perfect and perfection, the name of the very tense that reports a deed brought to completion.
