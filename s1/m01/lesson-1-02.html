★ The Nominative — Latin's Naming Case
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned that endings carry meaning. Today you meet the family of endings that does it — and learn the first one by name.
Latin sorts a noun's possible jobs into five sets of endings called cases. Each case marks a different role. You will meet all five today so you know the map — but you will learn only the first and most important: the nominative, the case that names the subject of the sentence. Learn to spot the nominative and you can always find who or what a Latin sentence is about.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…

I will be able to name the five Latin cases and state that each one marks a different job.
2

3
I will be able to find the nominative subject of a simple Latin sentence.

📚 PART 2 · THE FIVE CASES 11 min

A map of the jobs a noun can do

A Latin noun can play several roles — it can be the doer, the thing done to, the owner, the receiver, and more. Latin gives each role its own set of endings and calls each one a case. There are five, and here is the whole map. You are only responsible for the highlighted one this module; the rest are a preview of the year ahead.

CaseJob it marksTaught in
Nominativethe subject — the doer / the one the sentence is aboutthis module (M01)
Genitivepossession — “of,” whoseModule 06
Dativethe indirect object — “to” or “for” whomModule 07
Accusativethe direct object — the thing acted uponModule 03
Ablative“by, with, from” — and many prepositionsModule 08

Do not memorize the whole chart yet. Just take away the shape of the system: five cases, five jobs, five sets of endings. When you truly know all five, you can read almost any Latin sentence. We begin with the one you meet first in every sentence — the subject.

📌 PART 3 · THE NOMINATIVE = THE SUBJECT 9 min

The case that names the star

The subject of a sentence is the person or thing the sentence is about — the one doing the being or the acting. In Latin, the subject stands in the nominative case. Look at these simple sentences. (The little word est means “is” — it is a verb you'll formally learn in Module 02; for now just read it as “is.”)

Italia terra est.

Italy is a land. — Italia is the subject (nominative): the sentence is about Italy.

Britannia īnsula est.

Britain is an island. — Britannia is the subject (nominative).

Trōia patria est.

Troy is a homeland. — Trōia is the subject (nominative); this is Aeneas's home city, as you'll see this week.

💡 A HANDY TEST

To find the subject, ask: who or what is this sentence about? In Italia terra est, the answer is Italy — so Italia is the nominative subject. All eight of this module's nouns end in -a in the nominative singular, which makes the subject easy to spot for now.
