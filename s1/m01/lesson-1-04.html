⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 10 min

Sentences you can now read

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
You are about to read your first real Latin sentences. When you meet one, where will you look first to find the subject?

Read each aloud. Find the subject — the noun in the nominative — then read the gray parse line to check yourself. Remember, est just means “is.”

Trōia patria est.

Troy is a homeland. — Trōia nominative (subject) · patria the word it is equated with · est “is” (a verb formally taught in Module 02).

Italia terra est.

Italy is a land. — Italia nominative (subject). This is the land Aeneas is fated to reach.

Britannia īnsula est.

Britain is an island. — Britannia nominative (subject) · īnsula “island.”

Graecia patria est.

Greece is a homeland. — Graecia nominative (subject); the homeland of the Greeks who besieged Troy.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ THE TROJAN WAR & THE FALL OF TROY

The whole story begins with a theft. Paris, a prince of Troy — a great city on the coast of what is now Turkey — carries off Helen, the wife of a Greek king. To win her back, the kingdoms of Greece unite and sail against Troy. For ten years the two sides fight beneath the city walls; heroes fall on both sides — Hector for Troy, Achilles for Greece.

At last the Greeks win not by strength but by a trick: they build an enormous wooden horse, hide soldiers inside, and pretend to sail home. The Trojans drag the horse within their walls in triumph. That night the hidden Greeks climb out, throw open the gates, and burn Troy to the ground. The Trojan Horse is now the world's most famous ambush.

From the flames one Trojan prince escapes: Aeneas, son of the goddess Venus. He carries his aged father Anchises on his shoulders, leads his small son by the hand, and rescues the household gods of Troy. He is not running away — he is carrying Troy's future with him. He is destined to sail west and plant the seed of a new and far greater city. That city will be Rome.

⚖️ ROME, GREECE, AND FĀTUM

Rome and Greece. Centuries later Rome would conquer Greece by the sword — and then fall in love with everything Greek: its art, its poetry, its gods. Romans retold Greek myths as their own and answered the Greek poet Homer with their own poet Virgil, whose Aeneid follows Aeneas from Troy to Italy. To be Roman was, in part, to be an heir of Troy and a student of Greece.

Fātum — fate. Aeneas does not choose his journey; fātum chooses it for him. The word comes from fārī, “to speak” — fate is literally “what has been spoken” by the gods. Aeneas is fātō profugus, “an exile driven by fate.” A Roman hero's greatness lay in accepting the destiny the gods had spoken over him. (Etymology: fātum → fate, fatal.)

📜 PART 3 · ETYMOLOGY MOMENT 5 min

The grammar words are Latin too

Even the words we use to talk about Latin are borrowed from Latin. Here are the three from this module:

🌱 INFLECT · CASE · NOMINATIVE

inflect ← īnflectere = in- (in, toward) + flectere (to bend). To inflect a word is literally to “bend” its ending. The same root gives flexible, reflect, and deflect — and an “inflection” in your voice is a bend in its pitch.

case ← cāsus = “a falling,” from cadere (to fall). The Romans pictured the nominative as standing upright and the other cases “falling away” from it into their different jobs. The same root hides in cadence, accident, and occasion.

nominative ← nōmināre (to name), from nōmen (name). It is the “naming” case — it names the subject. The same nōmen gives English noun, nominate, nominal, and nomenclature.
