Lesson 1.01
★ The Secret Is in the Endings
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Before you learn a single Latin sentence, learn the one rule that makes Latin work — and it is not the rule your own language uses.
In English, a word's job is fixed by where it sits: “the dog bites the man” and “the man bites the dog” use the same words but mean opposite things, purely because the order changed. Latin does something else. In Latin a word tells you its job by how it ends — so you can slide the words around and the meaning holds. That single idea is called inflection, and today there is nothing to memorize and nothing to drill. There is just one door to walk through: endings carry meaning. Everything else this year is on the other side of it.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

Two languages, two strategies

Every language has to answer one question in every sentence: who is doing what to whom? English and Latin answer it in completely different ways.

English — uses word order

The word before the verb is the subject; the word after it is the object. Move the words and you change the meaning. Position is the code.

Latin — uses endings

A word wears an ending that announces its job. You can move the word anywhere in the sentence and its job stays the same. The ending is the code.

Changing a word's ending to show its job has a name: inflection. When we “inflect” a word, we bend its ending to fit the role it is playing. A language like Latin, which leans heavily on this, is called an inflected language. English inflects only a little (we add -s for plurals, -ed for the past). Latin inflects almost everything — and that is its superpower.

💡 WHY THIS IS GOOD NEWS

Because endings carry the meaning, Latin word order is flexible — a Roman poet could arrange words for beauty or emphasis without confusing anyone. Once you learn to read endings, you are no longer trapped by position. You read the information in each word directly.

✅ PART 3 · SEE IT ONCE, THEN CHECK YOURSELF 15 min

A tiny preview of the code

Here is the idea in miniature — just to see, not to memorize. Take the Latin word terra (“land”). Its ending can change to show its job:

FormEndingJob it announces
terra-a“land” as the subject — the doer
terram-am“land” as the object — the thing acted upon (an ending you'll formally learn in Module 03)

Same word, same position possible — but the ending tells you whether the land is doing something or having something done to it. That is inflection at work. You do not need to produce these yet; you only need to believe the principle: the ending is where the meaning lives.
