⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 12 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject (nominative), then the verb, and check that they agree in number. Some sentences use a first conjugation verb and a second conjugation verb together — watch the stem vowels. The gray line is your parse and translation; try it yourself first.

Populus virum videt.

The people see the man. — populus nom. sg. (subject) · virum acc. sg. (object) · videt 3rd sg., 2nd conj.

Vir in forō populum monet.

The man advises the people in the forum. — vir nom. sg. · in forō ablative phrase (“in the forum,” case from Module 8) · populum acc. sg. · monet 3rd sg.

Rōmānī glōriam amant et perīculum timent.

The Romans love glory and fear danger. — Rōmānī nom. pl. · amant 3rd pl., 1st conj. · timent 3rd pl., 2nd conj. One sentence, both families.

Servus dominum timet et labōrat.

The slave fears the master and works. — servus nom. sg. · dominum acc. sg. · timet (2nd conj.) + labōrat (1st conj.), both 3rd sg.

Discipulī magistrum vident, et magister discipulōs monet.

The students see the teacher, and the teacher advises the students. — discipulī nom. pl. + vident 3rd pl.; magister nom. sg. + monet 3rd sg. · magistrum / discipulōs are the accusative objects.

💡 READING TIP · TWO STEM VOWELS

When a sentence mixes families, the stem vowel is your quickest label: amant has the a of the first conjugation, timent the e of the second. The ending -nt tells you “they” either way — the vowel just tells you which family you are in.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 14 min
🏛️ WHAT IS RHETORIC? · THE FORUM RŌMĀNUM

Rhetoric is the art of persuasion — using language deliberately and well to move an audience to believe, to feel, and to act. To the Romans it was not decoration. In a Republic that governed by debate and by vote, the man who could stand up and persuade a crowd held real power. Armies won territory; words won the argument about what to do with it.

The stage for that power was the Forum Rōmānum — the open square at the heart of the city, ringed by temples, law courts, and the Senate house. From a raised platform called the Rostra, speakers addressed the people directly. A young Roman with ambition trained for years in rhetoric, because a great speech in the Forum could launch a career, win a trial, or turn the Senate against an enemy.

The voices that shaped the Republic were, above all, voices — not just generals but orators. The greatest of them, Marcus Tullius Cicero, rose from a family with no political fame to the highest office in Rome almost entirely on the strength of his speaking. Next module you will hear him in action, at the most dangerous moment of his career. For now, remember the core idea: in Rome, to speak well was to be able to lead.

⚖️ PERSUASION AND RESPONSIBILITY

Rhetoric is a tool, and like any tool it can build or harm. The Romans knew a persuasive speaker could serve the truth or bury it. That is why their best teachers insisted the ideal orator was a “good man skilled in speaking” — vir bonus dīcendī perītus. The skill matters; the character using it matters more. You will practice speaking with care this quarter — the virtue of responsibility in every word.
