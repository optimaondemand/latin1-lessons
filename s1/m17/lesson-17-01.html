📚 PART 2 · THE FIVE CASES AT A GLANCE 10 min

Five cases, five jobs

A Latin noun changes its ending to show what it is doing in the sentence. That job is called its case. Over the semester you learned all five. Here they are together, each with the question it answers.

CaseJob in the sentenceEnglish clue
Nominativethe subject; the predicate noun after essewho / what does it?
Genitivepossessionof / ’s
Dativethe indirect objectto / for
Accusativethe direct object; object of some prepositionswhom / what receives it?
Ablativemeans, and object of prepositions like in, cum, exby / with / from / in

Read one line to feel it working: Rēx rēgīnae dōnum dat. — “The king gives a gift to the queen.” Rēx is nominative (subject), dōnum accusative (the gift given), rēgīnae dative (to the queen). Three cases, three jobs, one sentence.

📌 PART 3 · THREE DECLENSIONS SIDE BY SIDE 10 min

The same five jobs, three sets of endings

A declension is a family of nouns that share the same endings. You learned three. Every one of them still uses the same five cases — only the spelling of the ending changes. Here is a model noun from each, in the singular.

Case1st decl. terra (f., land)2nd decl. servus (m., slave)3rd decl. rēx (m., king)
Nominativeterraservusrēx
Genitiveterraeservīrēgis
Dativeterraeservōrēgī
Accusativeterramservumrēgem
Ablativeterrāservōrēge
💡 THE GENITIVE IS YOUR KEY

To tell which declension a noun belongs to, look at its genitive singular: -ae = 1st, -ī = 2nd, -is = 3rd. That is why the dictionary lists rēx, rēgis — the second form hands you the declension and the stem at once.
