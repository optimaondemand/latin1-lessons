★ One Verb, Six Persons

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned to recognize the six forms. Today you learn to choose the right one.
A Latin verb changes shape depending on who the subject is and how many there are — that's person and number. Learn those two ideas and the six forms of esse stop being a list and become a tool: sum for “I,” sumus for “we,” and so on. This is the single most memorized paradigm in Latin — and once it's yours, it powers grammar you'll meet all year.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the person (1st, 2nd, 3rd) and number (singular, plural) of a Latin verb.
2
I will be able to recite all six present forms of esse — sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt — with their meanings.
3
I will be able to explain why Latin can leave out the words for “I,” “you,” and “we.”

📚 PART 2 · PERSON & NUMBER 10 min

Two questions every verb answers

Before the paradigm, two words. Person tells you who the subject is; number tells you how many. Together they give six slots — three persons, each singular or plural.

Singular (one)Plural (more than one)
1st person (the speaker)Iwe
2nd person (the one spoken to)youyou all
3rd person (the one spoken about)he / she / itthey

Every Latin verb form belongs to exactly one of these six slots. When you name a verb, you name its slot: “est is third person singular.”

✅ PART 3 · THE PARADIGM 18 min

Esse in the present tense

Here is the full paradigm — the six forms laid out in their slots. This is the one you memorize.

SingularPlural
1st personsum — I amsumus — we are
2nd persones — you areestis — you all are
3rd personest — he/she/it issunt — they are
💡 MEMORY HOOKS

Say them in order — sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt — as a little chant; the rhythm sticks. Notice the two “s-then-t” plurals: sumus (we, ends -mus), estis (you all, ends -tis), sunt (they, ends -nt). Those three endings -mus / -tis / -nt will return on every Latin verb you ever learn.

⚠️ IRREGULAR ALERT

Esse is the most irregular verb in Latin — don't hunt for a tidy pattern in sum / es / est. Just memorize the six. The reward: these exact forms come back as helping words in later tenses, so the effort pays off again and again.

Now put the paradigm to work — five reps, reading forms and matching persons both directions. Keep the chart above in view if you need it; the goal is to make the six automatic.

📌 PART 4 · A LATIN SHORTCUT 8 min

The form already tells you “who”

Because each form of esse carries its person built in, Latin usually doesn't need a separate word for “I,” “you,” or “we.” The verb alone can be a complete sentence.

Nauta sum.

I am a sailor. — sum already means “I am,” so no separate word for “I” is needed.

Rēgīna es.

You are a queen. — es = “you are.”

Latin has words for “I” and “we” (ego, nōs), but they're optional — saved for emphasis. Usually the ending does the work.

Your turn: the sentence Deae sumus has no word for “we.” What does it mean, and how do you know? Say it, then reveal.

“We are goddesses.” The form sumus is first person plural — it already means “we are” — so the “we” is built into the verb. Deae (nominative plural) is what we are.
