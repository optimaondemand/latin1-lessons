Lesson 2.04
★ Two Nominatives, One Equation — Reading Aeneas
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Verb plus vocabulary equals reading. Today the pieces click together — and you meet the hero the whole quarter is built around.
First, one elegant rule: when esse links two nouns, both stand in the nominative — the second one is called the predicate nominative. That rule lets you read real Latin equation sentences. Then you meet Aeneas: a refugee from a burned city, carrying his people's gods toward a homeland he has never seen, driven by the Roman virtue of pietas.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a predicate nominative and explain why both nouns joined by esse are nominative.
2
I will be able to read and translate simple Latin equation sentences that describe the Roman world.
3
I will be able to explain who Aeneas is and what pietas means.

📚 PART 2 · THE PREDICATE NOMINATIVE 10 min

Both sides of the equals sign

Because esse equates rather than acts, it doesn't send its second noun into a different case the way an action verb would. Instead it creates an equation: A = B. Both nouns name the same thing, so both stay nominative. The noun on the far side of esse is called the predicate nominative.

💡 THE EQUATION RULE

Dīdō rēgīna est → Dido = queen → “Dido is a queen.” Both Dīdō and rēgīna are nominative because they point to one and the same person.

SentenceSubject (nom.)VerbPredicate nom.
Aenēās nauta est.Aenēāsestnauta
Italia terra est.Italiaestterra
Venus dea est.Venusestdea
Sicilia et Britannia īnsulae sunt.Sicilia et Britanniasuntīnsulae

Notice the last one: two subjects joined by et (“and”) are plural, so the verb is sunt (“are”) and the predicate noun is plural too, īnsulae (“islands”). Subject and verb still agree in number.

📚 PART 3 · READ & MEET AENEAS 14 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject, find the form of esse, and check the equation. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it.

Trōia patria Aenēae est.

Troy is the homeland of Aeneas. — Trōia nom. (subject) · patria pred. nom. · est 3rd sg. [Aenēae = “of Aeneas,” a form you'll meet in M06 — just recognize it here.]

Aenēās pius est.

Aeneas is devoted (dutiful). — Aenēās nom. · est 3rd sg. Here the word after esse describes him rather than renaming him.

Venus et Iūnō deae sunt.

Venus and Juno are goddesses. — two subjects → sunt (3rd pl.) · deae pred. nom. pl.

Nauta sum.

I am a sailor. — sum (“I am”) carries its own subject · nauta pred. nom.

🏛️ WHO IS AENEAS? · AENEID, THE STORY BEGINS

Aeneas is a prince of Troy. When the Greeks destroy the city, he escapes the flames carrying his aged father on his shoulders, leading his young son by the hand, and bearing the Penātēs — the sacred household gods of Troy. His wife is lost in the chaos. He has no city, no home, and no clear road; he has only a divine command: sail west and found a new nation that will one day become Rome.

His mother is the goddess Venus, who watches over him; the goddess Juno, queen of the gods, hates the Trojans and throws storm after storm in his path. Above them, Jupiter guarantees that fate will hold: Aeneas will reach Italy. The whole journey — from burning Troy to the future site of Rome — is the story of this quarter.

⚖️ THE VIRTUE OF PIETAS

Virgil's constant word for Aeneas is pius — and the virtue behind it is pietas: dutiful devotion to the gods, to one's family, and to one's destiny. It is not merely being religious; it is putting duty above personal desire. Carrying his father from the fire and his gods across the sea, Aeneas is pietas made into a man. When he finally says sum pius Aenēās, that one adjective tells a Roman everything about who he is.
